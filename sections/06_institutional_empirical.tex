\section{Institutional Interpretation and Empirical Implications}\label{sec:institution}

The model is motivated by an institutional class in which regional public organizations perform innovation intermediation while projects can also use priced commercial innovation ecosystems in large metropolitan areas. The examples in this section establish the plausibility of the primitives; they do not provide causal evidence for the model's comparative statics.

\subsection{Public and private innovation intermediation}

In Japan, CIC Tokyo describes a paid coworking and innovation community that connects startups with investors, government, professional service providers, and other participants; its official materials also describe use by people who normally work in local areas but need a Tokyo base.\footnote{CIC Tokyo, \href{https://jp.cic.com/office-space/co-working/}{\emph{Coworking}} and \href{https://jp.cic.com/en/news/launch-on-1-oct-2020/}{launch/company description}, official webpages. Accessed September 3, 2026.} SHIBUYA QWS is a membership-based co-creation facility with project and corporate membership categories, and its membership agreement expressly permits changes to membership plans and charges under specified operational or economic conditions.\footnote{SHIBUYA QWS, \href{https://shibuya-qws.com/membership}{\emph{Membership}} and \href{https://shibuya-qws.com/membership/agreement/}{membership agreement}, official webpages. Accessed September 3, 2026.} These examples make a priced metropolitan outside option and operator discretion over an effective access price institutionally plausible. They do not establish that the observed price is chosen by the exact profit function in \eqref{eq:privateprofit}.

The same institutional class is not uniquely Japanese. Level39 in London offers paid community and coworking memberships and describes connections to mentors, investors, and industry experts.\footnote{Level39, \href{https://level39.co/workspaces/community-membership/}{\emph{Community Membership}} and \href{https://level39.co/about/}{\emph{About}}, official webpages. Accessed September 3, 2026.} CIC locations in Rotterdam, Warsaw, Cambridge, and Boston similarly combine paid workspace access with innovation-community and partner-network functions. On the public side, Helsinki's Campus Incubators Programme describes a city role in funding and connecting university incubation activity to the wider innovation ecosystem, while Barcelona Activa operates municipal startup incubation and network-support programs.\footnote{City of Helsinki, \href{https://www.hel.fi/en/business-and-work/campus-incubators-programme}{\emph{Campus Incubators Programme}}; Barcelona Activa, \href{https://emprenedoria.barcelonactiva.cat/en/startup-lab}{\emph{Startup Lab}}. Official webpages, accessed September 3, 2026.}

These institutions need not be ecosystem-wide competitors. Public and private organizations may collaborate, share events, or provide complementary services. The model requires a narrower form of substitution: for a focal project choosing a primary intermediation route, improving one route may reduce use of another. This distinction allows ecosystem-level complementarity to coexist with project-level substitution.

\subsection{Homing and objective assumptions}

Project single-homing should be interpreted as a primary-route abstraction. A firm may participate in many networks, rent multiple workspaces, or maintain relationships with several intermediaries while a particular collaboration or commercialization project relies on one main route. The support side is treated differently: investors, experts, universities, professional service organizations, and public agencies can plausibly connect to more than one innovation ecosystem. The model therefore permits partner multihoming, without claiming that every observed partner simultaneously participates in every relevant hub.

The private profit objective is also reduced form. It selects a commercial operator class for which paid access and demand responsiveness are economically meaningful; it does not assert that all real-world innovation hubs maximize the one-period expression $p_Tn_T^F$ or lack ecosystem objectives.

\subsection{Model predictions}

The model's primary price prediction is
\[
p^*_{T,x_i}<0
\]
at the verified configuration. Stronger regional facilitation lowers demand for the private route, and the optimizing private intermediary responds by cutting its access price. This is a model prediction, not an observed historical fact about CIC Tokyo, QWS, Level39, or any other named institution.

A second implication concerns strategic responses across governments. When private pricing is relatively rigid, cross-side participation can make regional investments move in the same direction. Greater private pricing flexibility introduces a countervailing reallocation channel and can, under the conditions identified in Theorem \ref{thm:reversal}, reverse that response. Empirically distinguishing the two regimes would require data on regional facilitation, project routing, partner participation, and private access prices. Such a test lies beyond the present theory paper.
