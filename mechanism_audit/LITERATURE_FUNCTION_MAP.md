# Prior-Literature Function Map

| Literature family | Motivation / observed failure | Economic friction | Intermediary role | Typical formal/conceptual primitive | Typical outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Innovation intermediary / broker | knowledge, capabilities and partners are distributed across organizations | search, information, organizational boundaries | scan, broker, connect, recombine knowledge | brokerage/network position; often conceptual rather than equilibrium | knowledge sharing, innovation capability, project formation |
| Search intermediation | mutually beneficial partners cannot find each other cheaply | costly search / delay | centralized meeting technology | search cost, intermediary fee, clientele | trade/match incidence, intermediary entry/profit |
| Competing matchmakers | heterogeneous participants need sorting into productive matches | matching/sorting friction | attract and sort both sides | match value complementarity, prices, market assignment | sorting efficiency, platform structure, fees |
| Two-sided platforms | participation on each side raises value to the other side | indirect network externality | coordinate participation | cross-side network effects, platform prices, multihoming | market shares, price structure, platform competition |
| Innovation intermediation | users cannot accurately evaluate inventions | uncertainty / information asymmetry | pool expertise and screen inventions | signal/sorting expertise | investment/licensing efficiency |
| Technology transfer offices | firms cannot observe invention quality or find suitable researchers/projects | information + matching | reputation, search, matching | signal/reputation; search intensity; market tightness | licenses / TT matches / innovation transfer |
| University–industry collaboration | heterogeneous academics and firms have different ability/affinity | partner-selection / matching problem | institutional context supports collaborative pairing | two-sided matching, complementarity | assortative matching, research collaboration |
| Living labs / testbeds | top-down development misses user/context knowledge and real-world performance | experimentation/learning friction | convene stakeholders and enable tests | experimentation, pilots, real-world test environment | learning, validation, adoption |
| Regional/public innovation intermediary | private intermediary supply may be missing, especially in peripheral regions | institutional void / thin market | publicly supported broker/orchestrator | mostly empirical/conceptual | regional collaboration, knowledge transfer, SME capability |
| Coopetition among innovation labs | growing number of similar intermediaries creates rivalry while specialization/learning creates reasons to cooperate | service overlap + resource/knowledge complementarity | cooperate with and compete against peer intermediaries | mainly qualitative; no canonical equilibrium model identified | specialization, learning, competition for clients/utilization |

## Key literature findings

### Functional innovation-intermediary literature

Feser (2023 systematic review) builds on Howells' functional definition: intermediaries can scan/process information, combine/recombine knowledge, act as gatekeepers/brokers, test/validate innovations, commercialize, and evaluate cooperation. The same review finds the empirical literature fragmented; 43 of 65 retained studies use qualitative methods. It also notes that intermediaries are often publicly financed, particularly where private offerings are thin.

The 2026 systematic review of innovation-intermediary mechanisms further identifies knowledge translation, orchestration, IP/technology-transfer mediation and SME capability building, with mechanisms including information-asymmetry reduction, trust building, cognitive translation and temporal alignment.

These literatures support treating search, information, brokerage, coordination and trust as **microfoundations of intermediation**, not automatically as separate canonical model primitives.

### Formal matching/intermediary literature

- Gehrig (1993): costly search among heterogeneous agents; intermediaries reduce trading friction.
- Caillaud & Jullien (2003): competition between matchmakers with indirect network externalities and multihoming.
- Damiano & Li (2008): competing matchmaking markets sort heterogeneous participants when match value is complementary.
- Rochet & Tirole (2003): general platform competition with two-sided network externalities.

Therefore neither “matching,” “intermediary competition,” nor “network effects among matchmakers” can be claimed as novel.

### Formal innovation/technology-transfer literature

- Hoppe & Özdenören (2005): formal innovation intermediary between invention creators and users; uncertainty about invention profitability gives value to intermediary screening expertise.
- Macho-Stadler, Pérez-Castrillo & Veugelers (2007): TTO reputation/pooling reduces asymmetric information about invention quality.
- Calcagnini et al. (2016): matching model of heterogeneous firms and university researchers.
- Calcagnini et al. (2019): technology transfer is an endogenous matching outcome driven by TTO search costs and firms' project advertising; externalities can generate excessive/insufficient TT and complementarity/substitutability in market tightness versus technology stock.
- Banal-Estañol, Macho-Stadler & Pérez-Castrillo (2018): two-sided matching model of heterogeneous academics/firms with complementarity in collaborative research.

Thus productive matching and university/firm complementarity are themselves prior art.

### Why the target may still be structurally open

The reviewed formal models typically stop at one of the following layers:

- intermediary pricing/participation/matching;
- invention screening/licensing;
- academic–firm match formation;
- data intermediation to downstream firms.

The documented search has not located a model in which **decentralized regional governments endogenously provide multiple overlapping productive-matching intermediaries, peer hubs can expand one another's effective partner pools as well as cannibalize matching opportunities, and matched beneficiary firms subsequently compete strategically in a product market with regional-welfare feedback to public provision**.

That combination—not matching or intermediation by itself—is the candidate structural gap.
