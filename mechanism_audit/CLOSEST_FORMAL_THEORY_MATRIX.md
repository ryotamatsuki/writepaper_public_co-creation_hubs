## Top-five one-page specialization tests

### 1. Caillaud & Jullien (2003)

**NO — for the full target architecture.**

It already absorbs competing matchmakers, indirect matching-network effects, multihoming and service competition. To obtain the target model one must replace private price competition with decentralized regional public provision, make matches productive inputs into beneficiary firms, add a downstream strategic product market, and feed regional downstream welfare back to provision. Those are multiple new game layers, not a symmetry restriction or fixed-cost addition.

### 2. Damiano & Li (2008)

**NO — full architecture.**

It strongly absorbs heterogeneous complementary matching and competing matchmakers. It lacks public provision, cross-jurisdiction welfare and downstream beneficiary competition.

### 3. Hoppe & Özdenören (2005)

**NO.**

It establishes that formal innovation-intermediary theory exists and that uncertainty/screening can motivate intermediation. The intermediary solves invention-evaluation/sorting problems; the target productive matching, public regional provision and downstream rival-firm feedback are not a relabeling.

### 4. Calcagnini et al. (2019)

**NO.**

It is dangerously close to the “hub improves matching” submodule because TT is endogenous matching and depends on TTO search intensity/cost. But the target asks why *multiple regional public intermediaries themselves* are complements/substitutes once matched firms subsequently compete. That requires more than parameter specialization.

### 5. Ichihashi (2021)

**NO.**

It demonstrates that competing intermediaries can affect downstream firms, so “intermediary + downstream industry” is not novel. However the mediated object is nonrival personal data sold by profit-maximizing intermediaries; there is no complementary collaboration formation or decentralized regional provision.

## Strongest absorption threat

**Caillaud & Jullien (2003).**

Reason: it attacks the most tempting candidate mechanism directly. If the eventual contribution can be stated merely as “two matching intermediaries may compete while users benefit from a larger network,” the project is absorbed. The eventual formal theorem must depend on the public regional provision/downstream productive consequences, not on standard matchmaker network effects.
