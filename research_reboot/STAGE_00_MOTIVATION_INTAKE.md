## 1. Executive verdict

**Canonical verdict: GO.**

There is a researchable economic question behind publicly supported co-creation hubs, but it is not the legacy paper's claim that a hub creates local matching and should be bundled with an external pipeline. The defensible research object is **regional innovation intermediation as a potentially under-, over-, or mis-provided coordination technology**. The core unresolved issue is whether decentralized provision implements the socially efficient mix of local matching, capability building, and extra-regional knowledge access, and whether public support has a distinct welfare rationale rather than simply relabeling standard local-public-good or innovation-intermediary results.

The Stage 0 search also produces a strong anti-confirmation result: the literature does not support unconditional complementarity between local and external collaboration. Aarstad, Kvitastein, and Jakobsen (2016) report substitution or subtractive effects for smaller firms, while positive complementarity is concentrated among larger firms with greater absorptive capacity. Public-intermediary evidence from Japan and Italy also documents incentive misalignment and unintended effects. Therefore the legacy `hub + pipeline is good` conclusion is not preserved.

Stage 1 is warranted because the legacy model is a concrete mathematical object whose inherited propositions can be separated from the still-valid institutional question.

## 2. Phenomenon without explanation

The phenomenon, stripped of the legacy explanation, is:

1. Regional and local governments, universities, public agencies, firms, and nonprofit organizations establish or fund organizations/spaces that facilitate interaction among firms, entrepreneurs, researchers, public actors, and other participants.
2. These organizations perform heterogeneous functions: targeted introductions, networking events, technology and knowledge mapping, project facilitation, training, experimentation, community building, access to laboratories/equipment, and sometimes national/international brokerage.
3. The same broad policy object is described under different labels: innovation intermediary, innovation hub, living lab, cluster organization, innovation pole, coworking space, technology transfer organization, public innovation intermediary, regional innovation platform, or ecosystem orchestrator.
4. Peripheral or organizationally thin regional innovation systems often have fewer specialized private intermediary services and weaker organizational support structures than metropolitan or thick systems.
5. Public funders frequently evaluate intermediaries with measurable short-horizon outputs even though learning, capability, network, and innovation outcomes can materialize later and can be hard to measure.
6. Local and extra-regional collaboration can both support innovation, but their interaction is empirically heterogeneous rather than universally complementary.

None of these observations implies that a public hub is efficient, that a physical space is necessary, or that public provision dominates private intermediation.

## 3. Legacy explanation separated from phenomenon

The legacy paper imposes the following explanatory architecture:

- a hub raises local matching in region A;
- repeated local matching creates period-2 redundancy;
- a pipeline creates extra-regional renewal;
- pipeline effectiveness is conditional on the hub;
- a short-run private/local evaluation differs from social welfare;
- therefore a hub can be dynamically inefficient alone but efficient when bundled with a pipeline.

These are **model hypotheses**, not features of the observed phenomenon. Stage 0 does not preserve them.

## 5. Actors / choices / frictions / outcomes

### Potential agents

- regional/local governments;
- firms and entrepreneurs;
- universities and research organizations;
- public or private intermediary organizations;
- extra-regional firms/researchers/experts;
- higher-level government as grant maker;
- hub/intermediary manager where agency problems matter.

### Potential endogenous choices

- whether to participate/search locally;
- whether to form extra-regional links;
- whether and how much to fund intermediation;
- whether an intermediary is public, private, mixed, or absent;
- intermediary effort allocation across measurable outputs versus long-horizon capability/network outcomes;
- regional investment in local versus extra-regional connectivity.

### Welfare-relevant outcomes

- match formation and match quality;
- knowledge diffusion and recombination;
- capability/absorptive-capacity formation;
- access to extra-regional knowledge;
- duplication/crowding out of private intermediaries;
- fiscal costs;
- distribution of benefits across jurisdictions;
- innovation and commercialization outcomes.

## 6. Preliminary literature-family map

### A. Regional innovation systems and organizational thinness

Cooke, Uranga, and Etxebarria (1997) establish the regional innovation-system perspective. Tödtling and Trippl (2005) explicitly distinguish peripheral, old-industrial, and fragmented metropolitan systems and reject one-size-fits-all innovation policy. Oughton, Landabaso, and Morgan (2002) identify a regional innovation paradox: lagging regions may need more innovation support while possessing lower absorptive capacity.

**Implication:** `thinness` is prior conceptual art, not a contribution.

### B. Local buzz and global/extra-regional pipelines

Bathelt, Malmberg, and Maskell (2004) provide the canonical local-buzz/global-pipelines framework. Subsequent empirical work strongly weakens an unconditional complementarity claim. Aarstad et al. (2016) find substitution/subtractive interaction for smaller firms and complementarity only for large/very large firms. Wang et al. (2023) find nonlinear effects of external linkages and complementarity between two kinds of external technology pipelines.

**Implication:** a model that simply formalizes `local buzz + global pipelines` is at high risk of being an old conceptual result with equations.

### C. Innovation intermediaries

Howells (2006) is foundational. Feser (2023) systematically reviews intermediary roles and stresses public funding and the lack of private intermediary offers in peripheral regions, while also noting the difficulty of evaluating resource efficiency. The 2026 review on innovation intermediaries in low-maturity systems identifies translation, information-asymmetry reduction, trust building, temporal alignment, IP/risk management, and capability building as mechanisms.

**Implication:** brokerage/matching alone is already well established. A new theory must produce a distinct strategic or welfare result.

### D. Living labs and co-creation

Fauth, De Moortel, and Schuurman (2024) conceptualize living labs as regional innovation-ecosystem orchestrators. Gizińska and Lankauskienė (2026) explicitly conceptualize living labs as adaptive intermediary structures embedded in peripheral regional innovation systems. Gibert and Plantec (2026) show that intermediary roles can generate legitimacy problems.

**Implication:** the institutional motivation remains current, but the conceptual territory around `co-creation intermediary in peripheral RIS` is increasingly occupied.

### E. Agglomeration, proximity, coworking

Boschma (2005) emphasizes that proximity has multiple dimensions and can produce lock-in when excessive. Roche, Oettl, and Catalini (2024) causally identify localized knowledge spillovers in a technology coworking hub and find effects that decay quickly with distance; the strongest spillovers occur among firms that socialize while being dissimilar.

**Implication:** local interaction value is evidence-based, but the public-welfare case is not established by proximity evidence.

### F. Public-intermediary incentives and implementation failure

Russo, Caloffi, Rossi, and Righi (2019) show that publicly funded innovation intermediaries can respond to short-horizon/easy-to-measure performance indicators in ways misaligned with policy objectives. Fukugawa (2022) finds an unintended negative treatment effect of incorporation on licensing income for Japanese Kohsetsushi. Fukugawa (2024) further documents heterogeneous treatment effects.

**Implication:** the legacy idea of a short-run/long-run wedge has institutional support, but the economically natural formulation may be **principal–agent / performance-measure distortion**, not an exogenously myopic private evaluator.

### G. Fiscal federalism and local public goods

Cremer, Marchand, and Pestieau (1997) directly compare Nash investment by two communities in an indivisible local public good with interjurisdictional spillovers against the social optimum. Wellisch (1993) shows that mobility can under some conditions internalize spillovers and eliminate the standard underprovision result.

**Implication:** `two regions + spillover + Nash versus planner = underinvestment` is not novel by itself. Any surviving model needs an intermediation-specific endogenous margin or different strategic interaction.

### H. Cluster policy and anti-confirmation evidence

Rigorous cluster-policy evaluation does not show automatic firm-performance benefits. Nathan et al. (2022, Research Policy) find that the UK Tech City programme grew/densified clusters but had weaker/partial firm-performance effects, with some apparent effects pre-dating rollout. Cluster-policy reviews also emphasize evaluation difficulty and heterogeneous outcomes.

**Implication:** government failure, duplication, and policy ineffectiveness must remain live candidate mechanisms.

## 7. Candidate mechanisms

### M1 — Local search/matching externality

- Agents: firms/researchers/entrepreneurs.
- Private choice: search/networking effort and participation.
- Friction: costly discovery of complementary partners; decentralized agents do not necessarily capture all knowledge spillovers from formed links.
- Decentralized consequence: potentially too few valuable matches.
- Social consequence: underconnected innovation network.
- Why a hub may matter: lowers discovery/meeting costs or organizes introductions.
- Why a hub may be unnecessary: private brokers, online platforms, cluster organizations, or universities may supply the same service.
- Literature threat: Howells (2006), intermediary literature, matching/network economics.
- Kill evidence: close formal model already derives the same provision/welfare result.

### M2 — Interjurisdictional spillover in innovation-intermediation investment

- Agents: regional governments and regional firms.
- Private/public choice: regional spending on intermediation/connectivity.
- Friction: part of the benefit of a regional investment accrues to actors outside the funding jurisdiction.
- Decentralized consequence: possible underprovision.
- Social consequence: inefficient regional network architecture.
- Hub role: one implementation of intermediation capacity.
- Why hub may be unnecessary: grants or direct external-link subsidies could solve the same problem.
- Literature threat: Cremer et al. (1997), fiscal federalism/local public goods.
- Kill evidence: if the only new ingredient is the word `innovation`, contribution is cosmetic.

### M3 — Endogenous absorptive-capacity / capability-building externality

- Agents: firms, intermediary, regional government.
- Private choice: current participation/intermediation effort.
- Friction: present intermediation changes future capacity to recognize/use external knowledge; beneficiaries or future counterparties may not pay for that capability formation.
- Decentralized consequence: insufficient capability-building or misallocation toward immediately measurable matching.
- Social consequence: dynamic underinvestment or wrong mix of intermediation activities.
- Hub role: repeated structured interaction/training/translation can be one capability-building technology.
- Why hub may be unnecessary: specialized consultants/TTOs may provide it.
- Literature threat: absorptive-capacity and intermediary literatures.
- Kill evidence: formal prior art already derives the same dynamic wedge and policy result.

### M4 — Performance-measure / principal–agent distortion in publicly funded intermediation

- Agents: government principal, intermediary manager, firms.
- Choice: intermediary effort allocation across measurable outputs and long-run outcomes.
- Friction: outcome measurement is noisy/delayed; funding uses short-run proxies.
- Decentralized/organizational consequence: effort is distorted toward events, contacts, or easy outputs.
- Social consequence: apparent short-run success with lower long-run capability/network value.
- Hub role: public co-creation hubs are a concrete setting where the intermediary manager is evaluated.
- Why hub may be unnecessary: mechanism generalizes to all publicly funded innovation intermediaries.
- Literature threat: Russo et al. (2019) already documents the mechanism empirically and performance-incentive theory is mature.
- Kill evidence: existing theory already maps directly to intermediary effort allocation with no new interaction.

### M5 — Local/external connectivity as endogenous substitutes or complements

- Agents: regional actors/intermediary.
- Choice: local networking effort and extra-regional search/link investment.
- Friction: external knowledge may require local absorptive capacity, but local and external collaboration can also compete for managerial attention/resources.
- Decentralized consequence: wrong portfolio, with possible substitution or complementarity.
- Social consequence: inefficient mix of local and external link formation.
- Hub role: may affect one or both margins.
- Why hub may be unnecessary: portfolio choice can occur without a physical/public hub.
- Literature threat: Bathelt et al. (2004), Aarstad et al. (2016), Wang et al. (2023).
- Kill evidence: no new strategic interaction beyond assuming a cross-partial sign.

### M6 — Public intermediary crowd-out / duplication

- Agents: government, public intermediary, private brokers/platforms.
- Choice: entry/provision/effort.
- Friction: public subsidy can displace private intermediation; local political incentives can sustain redundant organizations.
- Decentralized consequence: too much public intermediation or inefficient provider mix.
- Social consequence: excess fixed cost and possibly weaker incentives/quality.
- Hub role: a publicly financed provider competes with substitutes.
- Why hub may be unnecessary: private supply may dominate in thick markets.
- Literature threat: public-private provision and industrial-policy literatures.
- Kill evidence: standard crowd-out model fully contains the result.

## 8. Anti-confirmation findings

1. **No universal local+external complementarity.** Aarstad et al. (2016) reject the standard complementarity proposition for SMEs and find subtractive/substitution effects in parts of the size distribution.
2. **External linkage is not monotone.** Wang et al. (2023) report inverted-U relationships for technology introduction intensity.
3. **Public intermediaries can perform worse under institutional reform.** Fukugawa (2022) reports negative treatment effects of incorporation on Kohsetsushi licensing income.
4. **Performance targets can distort intermediary behavior.** Russo et al. (2019) document misalignment between target indicators and policy goals.
5. **Cluster support can densify a cluster without strong firm-level performance gains.** Rigorous cluster-policy evidence warns against assuming that more networking/activity equals welfare improvement.
6. **Mobility/other equilibrium responses can neutralize spillover underprovision.** Wellisch (1993) is a direct warning against mechanically equating spillovers with a public-policy wedge.
7. **Living labs/intermediaries face legitimacy/governance costs.** Gibert and Plantec (2026) show that multiple intermediary roles can undermine cognitive legitimacy.
8. **Private/non-hub substitutes exist.** Howells' broad intermediary category, digital brokerage, coworking, cluster organizations, universities, TTOs, and private knowledge-intensive services mean that `hub` is not the primitive.

## 9. Theory vs empirical vs mixed route

**Recommendation: theory-first with an explicit empirical/institutional bridge; retain an empirical pivot option.**

A pure descriptive paper is unnecessary because institutional and conceptual literatures are already extensive. A theory project remains justified only if Stage 2 finds an intermediation-specific strategic or welfare wedge that is not simply fiscal-federalism spillover, standard matching, or principal-agent theory with new labels.

The empirical route becomes preferred if Stage 2 kills all model/proposition-level distinctions but leaves a measurable policy question such as performance-indicator distortion, public/private intermediary substitution, or causal effects of hub participation/network structure.

## 10. Research-question competition

### RQ-A — public justification

> Under what conditions, if any, does publicly supported regional innovation intermediation improve welfare relative to decentralized private and local provision?

Strength: directly asks for a welfare rationale. Weakness: too broad and vulnerable to generic public-good/intermediary theory.

### RQ-B — decentralized portfolio

> Under what conditions does decentralized regional provision fail to implement the socially efficient mix of local innovation intermediation and extra-regional knowledge access?

Strength: creates an equilibrium-versus-planner object while allowing complements, substitutes, hub-only, external-only, or neither. Weakness: standard interjurisdictional spillovers are a major prior-art threat.

### RQ-C — dynamic capability and future matching

> When does current innovation intermediation change future matching/absorptive capacity in a way that decentralized regions or intermediaries fail to internalize, and what policy instrument implements the efficient allocation?

Strength: potentially creates an intermediation-specific dynamic state rather than an ad hoc novelty-loss term. Weakness: absorptive-capacity theory may already contain the mechanism; requires serious Stage 2 audit.

## 11. Selected one-sentence research question

**Selected RQ for Stage 1/2 audit:**

> **Under what conditions does decentralized regional provision of innovation intermediation fail to implement the socially efficient mix of local matching and extra-regional knowledge access, and does any resulting wedge depend on a genuinely intermediation-specific mechanism rather than a standard local-public-good spillover?**

This question deliberately does not assume underprovision, complementarity, a physical hub, or public superiority.

## 12. Main prior-art risks

1. Local buzz/global pipelines already supplies the conceptual local/external distinction.
2. Regional innovation systems already supplies `thinness`, lock-in, system failure, and differentiated policy.
3. Howells and subsequent intermediary work already supplies brokerage, matching, translation, and network support.
4. Cremer et al. already supplies strategic regional investment with spillovers, Nash equilibrium, planner comparison, and corrective grants.
5. Standard principal-agent/performance-measure theory plus Russo et al. may absorb the `short-run KPI versus long-run welfare` mechanism.
6. Existing network formation/search models may already contain underconnection due to spillovers.
7. Living-lab literature now directly frames co-creation entities as regional-system intermediaries/orchestrators, including peripheral regions.

## 13. Stage 1 audit scope

Stage 1 must not design a replacement model. It will reconstruct and audit the legacy model to determine what survives as mathematics and what is merely imposed by primitives.

Mandatory destruction tests:

- `q(b)=q0+eta*b*h`: complementarity by definition;
- `m(1-delta*m)`: redundancy by reduced form;
- private short-run evaluation versus welfare without decentralized equilibrium;
- passive Region B;
- thresholds generated mechanically by fixed costs and pair counts;
- consistency of welfare across regimes;
- absorptive-capacity extension as a pure surplus reweighting rather than endogenous capability formation.

**GO**

Reason: there is a precise economic question, multiple nontrivial mechanisms and anti-mechanisms, a real welfare/equilibrium object, and a concrete inherited model to audit. The GO is not an endorsement of the old theory.

## 15. Next-stage contract

Stage 1 may:

- verify/correct the inherited mathematics;
- classify every legacy proposition as correct, ad hoc, incorrect, or ambiguous;
- identify which residual economic questions survive destruction of the legacy assumptions.

Stage 1 may **not**:

- introduce a strategic Region B;
- make pipeline policy available without a hub as a repair;
- endogenize absorptive capacity;
- add private intermediary entry;
- add principal-agent contracting;
- change functional forms to obtain a preferred result.

Those are possible later mechanisms only if Stage 2 leaves room for them.
