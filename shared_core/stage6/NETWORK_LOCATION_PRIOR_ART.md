# Network-Location / Installed-Base Prior-Art Audit

## Question

Is the hardened theorem already contained in classic or current network-entry theory once public/regional labels are stripped?

The hardened theorem is **not** the broad statement that network effects or compatibility alter entry. It is the narrower location result:

- two named entrants draw users from a separate residual core;
- both entrants' user populations face the same residual core as an outside option;
- the residual core's value depends on the mass of users remaining there;
- core-side feedback can reverse the entrant-to-entrant cross-entry effect;
- entrant-side feedback alone does not generate that reversal in the canonical class.

## 1. Amir, Evstigneev & Gama (2021)

This is the most important new Stage-6 threat.

Their model contrasts:

1. firm-specific proprietary incompatible networks; and
2. a compatible single-network industry.

Proposition 3 and the accompanying discussion state that, starting from monopoly, industry viability **increases with more firms in the single-network model and decreases with more firms in the firm-specific-network model**.

### What this absorbs

It absorbs any broad contribution claim that:

- `network-effect location/architecture changes the effect of entry`;
- `compatibility/common network can turn entry from harmful to helpful`;
- `business stealing versus network effects can produce opposite entry implications`.

### Why it does not absorb the hardened theorem

Their single network is **jointly enlarged** by the firms operating in the industry. Additional firms contribute to the common network/market viability. The candidate residual core instead belongs to a distinct incumbent option: peripheral entry **reduces** the installed base of that core. The second entrant can therefore help the first not by enlarging a shared entrant network but by weakening their common outside option.

Their outcome is industry viability conditional on the number of firms, not a named bilateral entry externality `Gamma_i(E_j)`.

**Disposition:** broad location claim killed; residual-core theorem survives.

## 2. Amir & Lazzati (2011)

The single-compatible-network ancestor establishes that more firms can improve viability under network effects. This is direct prior art for entry-supportive common-network feedback, but the common network is the network of the firms themselves, not a third residual incumbent/outside option.

## 3. Gama (2019); Gama et al. (2020); Gama & Samano (2021); Basak (2021)

This sequence of papers establishes a mature body of theory in which:

- firm-specific versus compatible networks materially change entry incentives;
- network effects can produce counterintuitive output/profit/welfare responses to entry;
- free entry can be excessive or insufficient;
- compatibility can alter the socially desirable number of firms.

No inspected result defines two peripheral entrants whose entry decisions jointly deplete a third residual network, and none establishes the core-only versus entrant-only network-feedback sign diagnostic.

## 4. Economides (1996)

Strong network effects can give a quantity leader incentives to invite, license, or subsidize follower entry. This is strong prior art for the proposition that entry can benefit an incumbent when participants contribute to a common network. It does not supply the candidate entrant-to-entrant externality around a separate residual core.

## 5. Church & Gandal (1996) and installed-base entry deterrence

Installed complementary products can serve as an incumbent installed base and affect entry/standardization. The installed base is strategically important, but the model is incumbent-versus-one-entrant rather than two peripheral entrants sharing an endogenous residual outside option.

## 6. Feng & Hu (2024)

A large pool of potential firms makes entry decisions, then quality investments, while consumers arrive sequentially and create historical-sales network effects. Network strength produces phase changes in concentration/product variety. This is current prior art for nonmonotone entry under network effects, not for the candidate network-location necessity theorem.

## 7. Campbell, Ushchev & Zenou (2024)

Consumer social-network density changes the viability of a single entrant, generating blockaded, deterred and accommodated entry regions. Again, network structure produces entry-regime transitions, but there is no bilateral entrant cross-effect around a shared incumbent residual network.

## 8. Zhang (2026)

The current working-paper frontier studies an incumbent and one entrant with network spillovers, personalized pricing, consumer-network cohesiveness and interoperability. Entry profitability depends on cohesive/influential consumer bases and interoperability can discourage entry. It is an important incumbent/entrant network benchmark, but it lacks two named entrants and the diagnostic network-location theorem.

## 9. Bardey, Cremer & Lozachmeur (2014)

The phrase **common network externalities** is itself prior art in two-sided-market theory. Their common externality concerns both sides benefiting from changes in group sizes in platform competition. The paper has no entry stage that produces the candidate bilateral residual-core theorem. Therefore the project should not market `common network externality` as new terminology.

## 10. Bottom-line kill result

### Killed broad claims

- network structure/location affects entry;
- compatible/common networks can make entry beneficial;
- installed bases can change entry incentives;
- network effects can generate nonmonotone entry/viability outcomes.

### Surviving narrow claim

No inspected installed-base/network-entry paper establishes the following joint proposition:

> when two named entrants draw users from a distinct residual network that is their common endogenous outside option, feedback at that residual network can reverse their bilateral entry externality, while feedback confined to entrant networks alone cannot do so in the canonical class.

This narrow distinction is the only network-location novelty retained after Stage 6.
