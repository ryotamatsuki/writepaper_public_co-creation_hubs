# Generic Shared-Outside-Option / Common-Rival Kill

## Objective

Strip platform, region and public-sector labels and test whether the surviving theorem is merely a known `stronger common rival -> cooperation` or `endogenous outside option -> coordination` result.

## Common-enemy literature

De Jaegher (2021) surveys formal common-enemy mechanisms in which an outside threat increases cooperation. In the criticality/defense branch, changing threat intensity can change the underlying reduced-form cooperation game, including movement into Stag-Hunt/coordination structures. Contest models also allow competitors to stop competing with one another and pool effort against a stronger common enemy.

### Kill implication

The project may not claim any of the following as new:

- a stronger common rival induces cooperation;
- an outside threat can create coordination;
- a game can move from a noncooperative structure to a coordination structure as a common threat strengthens.

### Structural difference

The frozen candidate contains no direct defense payoff, coalition benefit, attack technology, threat criticality or reduced-form common-enemy complementarity. Entrant `j` affects entrant `i` only through user allocation: `j` attracts users away from a residual core; the core loses network value; the outside option faced by users considering `i` becomes weaker.

Thus the cooperation effect is not assumed in the payoff of the two entrants. It is induced by equilibrium movement in a third residual network.

## Endogenous-outside-option literature

Morales & Rodero-Cosano (2023) studies market entry with an endogenous outside option. The two players choose productive-market versus contest activity, with the outside-option rents linked to market profits; outside-option size affects pricing/coordination behavior.

### Kill implication

`Endogenous outside options affect entry and coordination` is not new.

### Structural difference

The outside option in that model is not a third shared residual product/network used by the same customer populations. One entrant's presence does not reduce an incumbent network's installed base and thereby change the other entrant's customer outside option. There is no core-only versus entrant-only network-feedback location diagnostic.

## Haller & Hoyer / network disruption

Common disruptors can alter network-formation incentives and stability. This is additional prior art against any broad common-threat/network-coordination headline. The disruptor channel is attack/disruption of links, not residual user diversion among a common outside option and two entrants.

## Generic formulation of the surviving mechanism

The theorem should be stated without the phrase `common enemy`:

> Two entrants compete against the same residual option. Entry by either entrant depletes the user base of that option, and the option's utility falls with its depleted user base. This endogenous weakening of the residual option can make the entrants strategic complements even though they also compete for users.

The hardened location result adds that, in the canonical class, putting network feedback only on entrant values does not generate the same complementarity.

## Verdict

- `stronger common rival -> cooperation`: **ABSORBED / NOT A CONTRIBUTION**.
- `endogenous outside option affects entry`: **ABSORBED / NOT A CONTRIBUTION**.
- `residual shared network outside option creates a bilateral cross-entry sign reversal, with a core-only/entrant-only location diagnostic`: **NO EXACT OR IMMEDIATE PRIOR ART IDENTIFIED**.

The remaining generic-isomorphism risk is substantive but not an unresolved evidence blocker after the targeted Stage-6 search. It should be tested for generality/economic importance in Stage 7 rather than rescued by new primitives.
