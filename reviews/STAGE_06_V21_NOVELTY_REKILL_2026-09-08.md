## 3. Proposition-by-proposition re-kill

### P1 — Two-sided/network interaction can make investment strategic complements

**KILLED AS NOVELTY.**

Farhi and Hagiu (2008), and later platform investment/pricing work, establish that two-sided network feedback changes strategic interaction. Tan, Anderson and Parker (2020) explicitly find platform-side integration investments can be complements. This ingredient is not new.

### P2 — Endogenous follower/private price response enters upstream investment incentives

**KILLED AS NOVELTY.**

López and Vives (2019) provide a generic multistage investment framework in which downstream product-market responses feed back into earlier R&D incentives. The existence of a `P_price`-type response channel is therefore not novel.

### P3 — Public investment competition can be strategically substitutable

**KILLED AS NOVELTY.**

Hindriks, Peralta and Weber (2008) derive downward-sloping public-investment best replies in a two-stage regional fiscal competition model. Sharma and Pal (2019) explicitly classify public investment as strategic substitutes while taxes are strategic complements. Kawachi, Ogawa and Susa (2015) likewise build interregional tax/public-investment timing results around the strategic nature of these policy instruments.

### P4 — A downstream strategic stage can qualitatively reverse an upstream public-investment distortion

**KILLED AS A BROAD NOVELTY CLAIM.**

Hindriks, Peralta and Weber (2008) explicitly state that introducing the strategic second-stage tax response can reverse the conventional over-provision conclusion for public investment. Dembour and Wauthy (2009) also study first-stage infrastructure investments followed by second-stage tax competition, with spillovers changing the infrastructure equilibrium. Thus, the broad statement that downstream strategic responses can overturn an upstream public-investment conclusion is prior art.

### P5 — A feedback institution can turn strategic complements into strategic substitutes

**KILLED AS A GENERAL NOVELTY CLAIM.**

Cornand and Heinemann (2022) show that a central bank's interest-rate response to aggregate prices can turn firms' pricing decisions from strategic complements into strategic substitutes. The abstract economic idea that an endogenous third-party/policy feedback can flip the strategic sign between other actors is therefore not new in general.

Sánchez-Cartas (2026) similarly shows that follower best-response feedback in sequential two-sided platform pricing can overturn a qualitative pricing ranking relative to the Armstrong benchmark.

### P6 — Public/private two-sided platform competition with endogenous private repricing

**KILLED AS SETUP NOVELTY.**

Liu, Rivadeneyra and Reshidi (2026) study a welfare-maximizing public payment platform competing with a profit-maximizing private platform in a two-sided market and show that the private platform can respond by changing fees. Public/private platform objectives and endogenous private repricing are therefore not sufficient for novelty.

### P7 — Matched-price public-public strategic sign reversal caused by shared private repricing

**SURVIVES.**

The re-kill did not identify a single prior model or theorem that reproduces the following complete comparison:

1. two decentralized public investors choose the same strategic variable in both environments;
2. both interact with a common profit-maximizing private platform/outside option;
3. B3 fixes the private price at the equilibrium G price, eliminating the response while matching the price level;
4. the public-public best-response slope is positive in B3;
5. restoring the shared private platform's optimizing price makes that same public-public slope negative in G;
6. the full-game reversal is supported by an all-regime global-equilibrium witness rather than only a stationary branch.

The closest fiscal-federalism papers have governments themselves choose taxes or infrastructure and do not supply this matched-price shared-private-follower comparison. The closest public/private platform paper has one public platform competing with one private platform, not two decentralized public investors whose mutual strategic relationship is the object. The closest generic follower-response papers establish the response channel but not this public-public matched-price sign comparison.

Classification: **proposition-level novelty / new result in a strategically distinct known-ingredient architecture**, not building-block novelty.

## 8. Surviving claim set

### S6-C1 — core contribution

Activating the optimizing price response of a shared profit-maximizing private two-sided alternative can reverse the strategic relationship between two decentralized public hub investments relative to a **price-matched fixed-price benchmark**: the same public actions are complements in B3 and substitutes in G.

### S6-C2 — identification contribution

Because B3 fixes the private price at the equilibrium G price, the comparison identifies the repricing-response margin rather than a difference in the level of the private price.

### S6-C3 — credibility upgrade from Stage 4/4A

The contribution is not supported only by an interior stationary point: the repaired model provides an independent all-regime computationally certified equilibrium witness with the opposite public best-response slopes.

### S6-C4 — mechanism support, not separate novelty

The decomposition `M_G = M_fixed + P_price` and the small-network local analysis explain how the repricing term can dominate the fixed-price cross effect. This is mechanism exposition, not a claim that follower-response terms are new.

> We do not introduce a new theory of two-sided platforms, public-investment competition, or follower-price responses. Our contribution is a narrower strategic comparison. Holding the shared private intermediary's price at the equilibrium level generated by the full game, decentralized public hub investments can be strategic complements. ## 10. Strongest remaining novelty threat

The strongest threat is the fiscal-competition literature beginning with Hindriks, Peralta and Weber (2008), because it already shows that first-stage public investment interacts with a second-stage strategic tax response and that the strategic stage can reverse a familiar investment conclusion. A referee can reasonably argue that the current paper is another application of a generic multistage-policy feedback principle.

The defense must therefore remain result-specific: those models do not establish a matched-price complement-to-substitute reversal of the **same public-public investment best-response slope** generated by a **shared profit-maximizing private intermediary**. If a paper is found that does establish that exact or directly isomorphic theorem, the contribution must be killed.

A secondary conceptual threat is Cornand and Heinemann (2022), which kills any generic claim that third-party feedback-induced strategic sign reversal is itself new.
