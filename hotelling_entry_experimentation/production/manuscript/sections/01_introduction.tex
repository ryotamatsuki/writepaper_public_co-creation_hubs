\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Can publicly supported innovation services extend regional firms' capabilities without displacing privately supplied expertise? This is not merely a hypothetical policy dilemma. In March 2026, a UK parliamentary committee cautioned that publicly backed Catapult centres might compete with local companies and universities for funding and projects, particularly in regions with weaker R\&D ecosystems \citep{UKCommittee2026}. In June, the British government defended the same network's mixture of public and commercial financing as a mechanism for keeping its activities responsive to industry and limiting private displacement \citep{UKGovernment2026}. The official positions differ on the incentives created by public support. Neither constitutes a controlled estimate of the net social effect of Catapults.

The European Digital Innovation Hubs programme makes the underlying price question similarly concrete: firms can receive free or discounted support, while the programme's operational pricing guidance asks providers to value subsidised services against market-price references \citep{EuropeanCommissionEDIH,EDIHFAQ2026}. Why may a zero-priced public option complement rather than replace specialist private provision? A small venture may have an attractive research idea but lack manufacturing or commercialisation capability; the US Manufacturing Extension Partnership reports precisely such an intermediary-assisted search for contract manufacturing by a Montana equipment startup \citep{NISTFiberReaper2024}. These institutions illustrate the problem but are not literal realisations of our stylised two-government/two-private-operator game.

We highlight three mechanisms that must be considered \emph{jointly}. First, a private specialist charges for premium services and selects founders on their willingness to pay. When that willingness is positively related to technical ability, service prices change not only \emph{how many} founders participate but also \emph{which capabilities} enter collaboration. Second, successful cross-region ventures generate private product profits and changes in consumers' and incumbent producers' surplus across different regional product markets. A new venture may benefit its founders while reducing incumbent rents. Third, local governments finance services from their own jurisdictions and maximise local welfare, whereas a national assessment must account for gains and losses in both locations. Consequently, the best private tariff, the local government's inclination to enter, and the nationally preferred provision profile need not align.

We construct a two-region model containing two independent local governments and two independent private intermediaries. All four choose binary provision decisions simultaneously; the active private intermediaries set fees, founders select services, cross-region pairs invest in a skill-complementary new-product research venture, and successful projects subsequently compete in differentiated Cournot product markets. The normal old-product industry operates even if no intermediation service is provided. The government's free basic service is a \emph{publicly financed service option}, not necessarily a publicly owned physical building.

The model delivers three conditional analytical mechanisms and one bounded numerical equilibrium illustration. With full participation and the fixed interior Cournot continuation, the marginal value of skill complementarity can be positive nationwide and negative in one region (Proposition~1). For an interior private-only service fee with the other region's participation fixed, stronger complementarity raises the optimal fee and the average skill of the selected users while lowering the mass and unnormalised skill moment of participation (Proposition~2). The welfare gain from adding free public service to an existing paid option differs between the local government and a restricted two-region planner by the marginal welfare accruing to the other jurisdiction (Proposition~3). In the documented baseline, increasing complementarity changes the decentralised configuration from co-provision in one region and private-only service in the other to co-provision in both, whereas equal-mean homogeneous success does not produce that switch. This is a numerical existence illustration, not a universal comparative-static or global equilibrium-uniqueness theorem.

Our contribution is deliberately \emph{not} the first demonstration of any of these ingredients in isolation. Free public and paid private competition is studied by \citet{HuaChenZhang2016,LaineMa2017,Kim2024}; endogenous platform fees and user participation by \citet{Hagiu2004,PeitzSato2026}; heterogeneous collaborative knowledge by \citet{BerliantFujita2008,BerliantFujita2011}; links and oligopoly by \citet{GoyalJoshi2003}; and the gap between local public provision and an interjurisdictional benchmark by \citet{CremerMarchandPestieau1997,LongWong2009}. Our narrower contribution is to \emph{couple} tariff-induced selection into complementary innovation, regional downstream business-stealing effects and independently chosen public/private provision \emph{within one tractable two-region game}. Section~\ref{sec:literature} explains what each predecessor already establishes and what the present specification adds. We do not claim a first-ever ownership reversal or a theorem that cannot be nested in any earlier model without full proposition-level verification.

Two distinctions limit what can be inferred. The restricted planner compares only the same sixteen provision configurations facing decentralised continuations; it does not implement a first-best research or service policy. Moreover, the skill--taste alignment, independent uniform skills, product-market structure and selected downstream price equilibria are material maintained assumptions. Negative portability checks for an alternative ability distribution and Bertrand competition are retained. The findings therefore diagnose the design and incidence of publicly financed innovation services, \emph{not} the universal necessity of building an innovation hub.

The remainder of the paper develops the model in Section~\ref{sec:model}, proceeds backward from private fees to provision incentives in Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium}, derives the conditional mechanisms in Sections~\ref{sec:propositions}--\ref{sec:welfare}, reports the numerical example and limitations in Sections~\ref{sec:numerical}--\ref{sec:robustness}, then positions the results in the literature and institutional setting before concluding.
