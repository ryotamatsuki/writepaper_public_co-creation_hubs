\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Local governments provide free basic innovation intermediation while private specialists sell paid services to prospective founders. The welfare case for public access depends on more than the number of supported ventures. Regions can differ in the population seeking services, the competitive effects of commercialisation on existing producers and consumers, and the share of new venture profits retained by local founders. Technical complementarity between research partners can also change the value of access. A common service policy can consequently face different regional economic boundaries, even without differences in the underlying distribution of founder abilities.

Several constituent mechanisms are already understood. Private matching prices select heterogeneous participants and can affect match quality as well as volume \citep{DamianoLi2007,DamianoLi2008,Hagiu2004b}. Public and private services can coexist, with endogenous private fees and participation \citep{HuaChenZhang2016,PeitzSato2026,LiuReshidiRivadeneyra2026}. Research complementarities and product-market rivalry affect innovation returns, while cross-jurisdictional benefits can separate local provision incentives from national welfare \citep{DAspremontJacquemin1988,CremerMarchandPestieau1997}. None of these mechanisms, taken separately, is a novelty claim of this paper.

We ask what determines the maximum fixed cost a local government is willing to bear to restore basic innovation access when a private specialist selects founders through its fee, and how the local evaluation differs from the national evaluation of the same provision margin. Our analytical comparison holds the partner region fully participating while allowing the local specialist to reoptimise. Regional venture-profit appropriation, consumer and incumbent incidence, and basic versus premium service value determine the boundary. Separate certified examples trace its consequences through the four institutional entry incentives and a national comparison of sixteen provision configurations. The examples complement the conditional analysis; they do not classify equilibrium across all regional market structures.

The model has two jurisdictions, each with a welfare-maximising government, a profit-maximising private intermediary and heterogeneous prospective founders. Four independent entry decisions precede private fees and participation. Cross-region pairs undertake research; successful ventures compete with incumbents in differentiated Cournot markets. A founder's technical ability equals her valuation of the premium service, and success includes the product of the partners' abilities. The private premium supplies direct service value rather than a productivity improvement. Governments value regional service benefits, consumers, incumbents and assigned venture earnings; locally paid fees are transfers. The public instrument is service availability, with no choice of buildings, subsidies or procurement.

First, private pricing selects founder composition. Conditional on partner participation, stronger skill complementarity raises the interior private-only fee and cutoff, increasing average participant ability while reducing participation and the total represented skill moment. Free basic access restores coverage alongside a paid premium. It reduces the specialist's conditional fee receipts, but entry remains profitable under the stated sufficient fixed-cost condition. These analytical results organise familiar screening and mixed-provision mechanisms rather than establish a new screening theory.

Second, the conditional public-cost ceiling combines direct service gains, baseline matched-project returns and skill-related commercialisation incidence. Proposition~1 identifies a primitive profit-share band in which technical complementarity raises that ceiling even though successful ventures have a negative net regional market effect. The local founder share must be large enough to generate private exclusion, yet small enough that consumer and incumbent effects dominate the assigned venture profits. Basic-service value relative to premium value lowers the critical share under the proposition's restrictions. The sign result holds analytically for sufficiently weak positive matching, uniformly on a fixed feasible complementarity interval. A separate exact-rational certificate verifies negative commercialisation incidence, positive access value and a positive value slope on a continuous two-parameter region at the original finite matching intensity. The share band can be written as
\[
 \omega_i^{\mathrm{crit}}<\omega_i<
       \min\{1,-M_i/\Pi\}.
\]
The condition identifies a policy evaluation error: a negative regional return to commercialisation does not determine the sign of the value of restoring basic access when private fees respond to technology. The result concerns this conditional policy margin, rather than a new general screening mechanism.

Third, local and national public-cost ceilings differ by the other region's welfare increment. The familiar externality wedge here operates through endogenous fees, selected abilities and regionally assigned commercialisation returns. In the three certified baseline environments, the national welfare maximum over sixteen configurations is always $(B,B)$, where $B$ means basic public access plus private premium provision. Decentralised provision is $(B,P)$ with skill-based success at zero complementarity, $(B,B)$ at complementarity $0.5$, and $(B,P)$ with the equal-full-participation-mean pooled technology at $0.5$; $P$ means private only. These are exactly certified examples, not a general equilibrium comparative-static theorem. The three-way pattern requires a narrow public-cost band and disappears in the recorded Beta$(2,2)$ ability alternative. Public overprovision is not established by these examples.

The contribution is a conditional public-access comparison that joins two distinct effects of the same technological change: private exclusion and the regional incidence of restored collaborations. The share band and service-value boundary state when those effects produce opposing policy signals. The full-game examples illustrate one consequence without supplying quantitative programme guidance.
