\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

When private specialists already sell support for collaboration and commercialisation, what is the economic case for a local government to provide basic innovation intermediation at no user charge? The European Digital Innovation Hubs network illustrates a setting in which publicly supported access to technical expertise and experimentation can coexist with services valued at market prices \citep{EuropeanCommissionEDIH,EDIHFAQ2026}. Such coexistence raises a question of policy appraisal, not merely institutional ownership: how large a real fixed cost can a local government justify for keeping basic access available? Counts of assisted firms or successful ventures alone do not answer it, since access decisions and the regional incidence of venture returns both matter.

The question becomes less straightforward when research partners have complementary technical skills. Greater complementarity makes potential collaborations more valuable, but a fee-setting specialist can respond by charging more and serving a smaller, more selected group of founders. Offering free basic access alongside a paid premium then restores participation excluded by the private-only fee, while also changing the use of premium services. Meanwhile, successful ventures may displace incumbent profits or generate benefits outside the financing jurisdiction. A technological improvement can therefore increase the benefits recovered by public access while also worsening the local market return from the additional collaborations. The balance between these forces, rather than either effect in isolation, determines the change in the value of free access.

The constituent mechanisms are well established. Private matching fees can screen heterogeneous participants and alter both participation and composition \citep{DamianoLi2007,DamianoLi2008,Hagiu2004b}. Free public and paid private services may coexist with endogenous private fees \citep{HuaChenZhang2016,PeitzSato2026,LiuReshidiRivadeneyra2026}. Innovation spillovers and product-market rivalry also separate local and aggregate welfare \citep{DAspremontJacquemin1988,CremerMarchandPestieau1997}. We do not claim these ingredients as new. Instead, we ask how technical complementarity changes the maximum fixed cost of adding free basic access when the private intermediary reoptimises its fee, and why the resulting policy-value response can have the opposite sign from local returns to commercialisation. The question concerns a specific public-access margin, not a general theory of screening or an unrestricted comparison of policy instruments.

We examine two jurisdictions, each containing a welfare-maximising government, a profit-maximising private intermediary and heterogeneous prospective founders. Four independent provision decisions precede private fees, founder participation, cross-region joint research and differentiated-product Cournot competition. The tractable specification assumes uniformly distributed technical ability, perfectly aligned with willingness to pay for the premium service. The premium provides additional direct service value, not an improvement in research productivity. Each government can supply a zero-priced basic service at a real fixed cost; neither government chooses a building, subsidy or procurement contract. Our central analytical comparison holds participation in the partner region at full coverage while allowing the local private fee to adjust. These restrictions are material to the result.

Private-only pricing first selects the composition of founders: on the interior branch, greater skill complementarity raises the optimal fee and exclusion cutoff, so the average ability of participants rises while the number participating falls. Free basic access restores participation alongside the private premium, although it reduces the specialist's conditional fee receipts. These selection and coexistence effects are preparatory mechanisms rather than the central novelty.

Proposition~1 identifies the distinct policy implication. The local founder's share of venture profits must be large enough for greater complementarity to strengthen private fee-induced exclusion, yet small enough for the region's net commercialisation incidence---including consumer, incumbent and assigned founder returns---to remain negative. Between these bounds, and under the proposition's conditions, greater complementarity raises the gross public-access cost ceiling despite adverse local commercialisation incidence. The positive slope is proved for sufficiently weak positive matching on a feasible complementarity interval, with a positive baseline success probability; an exact-rational certificate establishes the relevant signs over a continuous two-parameter region at the original finite matching intensity. This result concerns the direction of a conditional welfare boundary. It neither establishes that public provision is always desirable nor predicts how all four provision decisions change with technology.

A local government's cost ceiling may also differ from a national evaluation because additional collaborations affect the partner jurisdiction. This externality principle is familiar; here the welfare difference is evaluated with privately selected founders and regionally assigned venture returns. In three separately certified numerical environments, decentralised provision falls short of the restricted national welfare benchmark in two cases and coincides with it in one. The illustrated change in the four-party provision configuration requires a narrow fixed-cost interval and does not survive the recorded alternative Beta$(2,2)$ ability specification. The numerical results are therefore equilibrium witnesses, not a general classification or a calibrated estimate of programme effects.

The paper's contribution is a conditional policy boundary linking two responses to the same technical change: private exclusion and the regional incidence of restored collaborations. In particular, the sign of local commercialisation surplus alone does not determine the direction in which the value of basic access moves when private pricing adjusts. This is more specific than the familiar advice to include all benefits in a cost--benefit calculation, but it does not establish a general policy for building public innovation facilities.
