\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Can better research technology increase a local government's willingness to finance free innovation intermediation even when additional successful ventures reduce that jurisdiction's own economic surplus? A natural answer is no: if the projects supported by additional access impose more local losses than benefits, stronger technological complementarity should make access less valuable. That inference fails when successful ventures have private returns to founders and a fee-setting specialist selects which founders obtain access. Better matching technology can increase the specialist's optimal fee and exclude founders who would otherwise obtain valuable basic services. Public provision can then become more attractive because it restores \emph{access}, not because it raises the locally measured return to commercialisation.

We formalise this mechanism in a two-jurisdiction sequential game. In each region, a welfare-maximising government and an independent profit-maximising intermediary decide whether to provide a free basic service and a paid premium service, respectively. The four binary entry decisions are followed by private fees, endogenous founder participation, randomly formed cross-region research pairs, and differentiated Cournot product-market competition after innovation succeeds. Founders' private valuations of premium intermediation coincide with their relevant technical abilities, making higher fees a source of ability selection. Research success depends on the product of the two partners' abilities. Premium intermediation itself confers only a direct service benefit; it does not improve a founder's research productivity. A local government values basic-service access and the region's consumer, incumbent-firm and assigned venture outcomes, not the intermediary's fees as additional social income.

Our central result is a \emph{conditional screening--incidence inversion}. Holding the partner region fully participating and comparing a local private-only intermediary to free-basic/public-plus-private coexistence, we derive a sharp primitive criterion under which increasing bilateral skill complementarity raises the maximum public fixed cost that the government is willing to bear even though a successful venture's net regional product-market incidence is strictly negative. Write $M_i$ for regional consumer-plus-incumbent surplus changes from a successful venture, $\Pi>0$ for its aggregate operating profits, $\omega_i$ for the fraction assigned to the region's founder, $u_i>0$ for basic-service value, and $\Delta_i>u_i$ for incremental specialist value. For sufficiently weak positive matching, the inversion obtains when the venture-profit share lies between the screening threshold $\omega_i^{\mathrm{crit}}$ defined in \eqref{eq:omega-critical} and the regional loss threshold:
\[
 \omega_i^{\mathrm{crit}}<\omega_i<
       \min\{1,-M_i/\Pi\}.
\] The lower bound captures the endogenous screening/access-restoration mechanism, while the upper bound requires the region's net commercialisation return to be negative. The result is not the assertion that better innovation always justifies public provision; it identifies an economically interpretable second-best policy condition.

This primitive-level condition is an analytical strengthening of the welfare-threshold decomposition and derivative already implied by the model. The contribution is not a new theory of price screening, matching complementarities, local spillovers, or free public alternatives taken separately. Instead, it links their familiar mechanisms to a specific policy-value sign conflict: enhanced research complementarities can raise the value of recovering fee-excluded \emph{basic services} even while the induced ventures' regional market surplus falls. The result also admits a positive public-access cost ceiling when $0<u_i<2\Delta_i/3$ and matching is sufficiently weak. With the original finite matching intensity, an exact rational interval certificate verifies negative venture incidence, a positive public-access value and its monotonic increase on a continuous two-parameter set of profit shares and complementarities. It does not certify that the four institutions' equilibrium entry decisions switch on that whole set.

The distinction between a conditional welfare slope and an equilibrium ownership change is essential. In the original calibrated illustration, complementarity rising from zero to $0.5$ changes the strict first-stage equilibrium from $(B,P)$ to $(B,B)$; an equal-full-participation-mean homogeneous success technology instead retains $(B,P)$. Here $B$ denotes free-basic/public-plus-private provision and $P$ private only. The restricted two-region planner chooses $(B,B)$ in all three settings. All reported baseline fee continuations and first-stage deviations have analytical/exact-rational certificates, but the three-way ownership pattern survives only within a public-cost band about $0.2632\%$ of the benchmark cost, and the recorded Beta$(2,2)$ alternative does not reproduce it. The numerical switch illustrates rather than establishes the broadly applicable claim; the analytical sign condition is the primary economic result.

We position the analysis against work that already studies price-selected matching under complementary types \citep{DamianoLi2007,DamianoLi2008}, quality- and quantity-based platform exclusion \citep{Hagiu2004b,VeigaWeylWhite2017}, private-price and participation responses \citep{PeitzSato2026}, and mixed public/private service markets \citep{HuaChenZhang2016,AtalCuestaGonzalezOtero2024,LiuReshidiRivadeneyra2026}. Public/private R\&D finance and additionality are also established policy objects \citep{TakaloTanayamaToivanen2013}. Those studies differ in instruments, allocation rules or downstream payoffs; difference in modelling assumptions alone does not prove theoretical priority over their general mechanisms. The remaining contribution here is a restricted model-specific primitive sign condition with unusually clear consequences for local welfare measurement, not an independently established universal innovation-policy theorem.
