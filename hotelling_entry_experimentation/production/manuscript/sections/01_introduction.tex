\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Public innovation support frequently operates next to private specialist services. Does providing a free basic service strengthen collaboration or displace the private market that already serves technically capable firms? This tension has attracted explicit policy attention. A British parliamentary committee questioned whether publicly supported Catapult centres might compete with local institutions for funding and projects \citep{UKCommittee2026}, while the government emphasised the market discipline of their mixed public and commercial financing \citep{UKGovernment2026}. The European Digital Innovation Hubs network also offers subsidised technical services whose accounting may use market-based price references \citep{EuropeanCommissionEDIH,EDIHFAQ2026}. These observations motivate the question; they cannot establish either positive or negative causal effects of a public hub.

The answer may depend not only on how many firms use an innovation service, but on \emph{who uses it and where the returns to collaboration are realised}. Suppose a private intermediary charges a premium fee and firms' willingness to pay is associated with the technical skill they bring to a bilateral venture. Higher fees then reduce the number of participants while raising their average skill. A free public option admits additional firms, but these marginal participants need not possess the skills most valuable to the venture. Moreover, when the resulting new product succeeds, founders and consumers may gain while incumbent producers lose; the benefits and losses may fall in different jurisdictions. A local government paying the public fixed cost need not select the provision configuration that maximises two-region welfare.

We analyse this question in a sequential game with two jurisdictions, two local governments and two private service intermediaries. Each of the four parties independently decides whether to supply a basic public or premium private service, allowing sixteen initial configurations. Private entrants subsequently set posted fees; heterogeneous firms select into collaboration; skill-complementary cross-region pairs invest in a new-product venture; and the new product competes with incumbent producers in differentiated Cournot markets. Old-product production continues in the absence of intermediation. The government's zero-price service can be delivered through different organisational arrangements: we do \emph{not} model the need for a physical public facility.

The central tractable distinction is between the mass $m_i$ of collaborating firms and their unnormalised skill moment $q_i$. Their products $m_Am_B$ and $q_Aq_B$ separately govern match volume and skill-weighted innovation. We establish three \emph{conditional} relations: the regional incidence of complementarity can have opposite signs across jurisdictions; interior private pricing can raise selected mean skill while reducing total participants and their aggregate skill moment; and the national-minus-local willingness to finance additional basic service equals the welfare change in the other jurisdiction. These are valuable accounting and mechanism results, but none is asserted to constitute a previously unknown general theorem.

What differentiates this particular specification is the \emph{joint} mapping from private-price selection to bilateral innovation quality, downstream market displacement and four autonomous provider decisions. However, the closest literature makes a broad novelty claim untenable. Mixed public/private service competition has been studied by \citet{HuaChenZhang2016,LaineMa2017,Kim2024}; knowledge composition by \citet{BerliantFujita2008,BerliantFujita2011}; local-public spillover inefficiency by \citet{CremerMarchandPestieau1997}; and two-government/two-private-provider rivalry by \citet{LongWong2009}. Most importantly, \citet{Hagiu2004b} already analyse vertical provider heterogeneity, quality investment and variety-versus-quality welfare rankings. A recent published platform paper by \citet{LuMatsushimaShekhar2025} and a public--private Hotelling discussion paper by \citet{KawasakiMatsushima2026} independently show that pricing and ownership can reshape welfare rankings. Our claim is therefore restricted to a model-specific integration, with theorem-level non-absorption from every predecessor still to be established (Section~\ref{sec:literature}).

The baseline numerical game illustrates that heterogeneous bilateral abilities can affect the local provision configuration even when the compared homogeneous technology preserves mean success under complete participation. However, the public-entry difference occurs only within a narrow interval of region-$B$ fixed costs: approximately $0.0400432$ to $0.0405317$, while the original fixed cost is $0.0405$. At the upper boundary, an increase of just $0.0001$ in that cost removes the original public-entry incentive. We report this sensitivity directly, rather than treating the resulting ownership change as a general robustness result. Negative checks under a Beta skill distribution and differentiated Bertrand competition further limit portability.

The paper consequently addresses a \emph{diagnostic} issue: when the pricing of specialist services selects technically complementary firms, what are the local and cross-jurisdictional welfare consequences of offering a free basic alternative? It does not establish that free services always dominate, that subsidies or contracts cannot replicate their benefits, or that a publicly owned building is needed. We present the model and backward-induction restrictions first, then the conditional mechanisms, the exposed numerical sensitivity, closest theoretical predecessors and institutional implications. The final assessment of journal-level novelty remains contingent on the exact absorption and robustness evidence.
