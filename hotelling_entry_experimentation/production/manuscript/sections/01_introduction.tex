\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Can free public innovation intermediation change the configuration of private specialist services when successful collaborations compete in product markets beyond the jurisdiction that finances the service? The policy question is not simply whether more founders participate. A private fee can exclude less willing-to-pay founders while selecting a group with higher technical ability; a free basic alternative admits more founders but changes the composition of collaborations. If the resulting ventures sell differentiated products, their gains to consumers and founders and their losses to incumbent producers need not accrue in the same jurisdiction. Public provision can consequently be privately unattractive, locally attractive, or nationally desirable for different reasons.

We study this mechanism in a two-region game with four independent binary entry decisions: two local governments may finance a zero-priced basic service, and two profit-maximising private intermediaries may supply a priced specialist service. Private entrants subsequently set fees, heterogeneous founders choose participation, randomly matched cross-region pairs undertake skill-complementary R\&D, and successful ventures compete against incumbent firms in differentiated Cournot markets. The analysis distinguishes the participation mass $m_i$ from the \emph{unnormalised} first moment of participating ability $q_i$. Match volume depends on $m_A m_B$, while the skill-related part of innovation success depends on $q_A q_B$. Pricing and public access therefore change two distinct economic aggregates before product-market payoffs enter the four providers' entry incentives.

The central result is a \emph{conditional provision-configuration contrast}, not a universal public-ownership theorem. In the maintained numerical environment, raising skill complementarity from $\lambda=0$ to $\lambda=0.5$ changes the decentralised configuration from $(B,P)$ to $(B,B)$, where $B$ denotes free basic and paid specialist services together and $P$ denotes private-only service. At $\lambda=0.5$, replacing skill-dependent success by a homogeneous premium with the \emph{same mean under complete participation} instead yields $(B,P)$. The restricted two-region planner selects $(B,B)$ in all three comparisons. This isolates how participation composition and its feedback through regional product-market incidence can matter even when the technology's full-participation mean is unchanged. It does not identify a causal effect in observed regions.

The mechanism is made transparent by three conditional analytical results. Private pricing can increase the \emph{mean} ability of participants while reducing both the number of participants and their aggregate skill moment. A successful innovation changes the two regions' consumer--incumbent surplus and assigned venture earnings differently. Adding free basic access changes local welfare by its direct service value and regional matching gains, while the difference between the local and two-region provision thresholds equals the neighbouring region's incremental welfare. These components are individually familiar; the paper's additional object is their equilibrium connection from fee-based skill selection to four independent provision decisions. For the three reported baseline environments, a separate analytic best-response argument and exact rational interval checks also validate the private/private fee continuation and first-stage entry comparisons; no parameter-global pricing theorem is asserted.

The contrast has narrow quantitative scope. Opposite local public-entry choices under heterogeneous and homogeneous technology require $0.040043229233<F_{G_B}<0.040531670102$ when other baseline parameters are held fixed, a band only about $1.21\%$ of the baseline public fixed cost. Those bounds describe the two-way heterogeneous-versus-pooled comparison at $\lambda=0.5$. Requiring the paper's additional $\lambda=0$ heterogeneous $(B,P)$ equilibrium narrows the strict common interval to approximately $(0.0404250710071,0.0405316701024)$, or only $0.2632\%$ of the fixed cost. An exact rational all-sixteen-profile certificate verifies the open interval and a small explicit three-dimensional neighborhood of two government costs and regional venture profit allocation. The actual baseline remains particularly close to the upper boundary. Under the separately tested $\operatorname{Beta}(2,2)$ skill distribution, the corresponding ownership switch is absent; the differentiated-Bertrand extension is not independently re-certified in this revision. These limitations prevent interpreting the example as a generic prediction that skill complementarity calls for more public provision.

The positioning relative to previous theory is essential. \citet{Hagiu2004b} already study vertically heterogeneous developers, quality incentives and the variety--quality welfare trade-off; \citet{HuaChenZhang2016} study free public and paid private tiers; \citet{LongWong2009} already combine two governments with private service competition; and \citet{PeitzSato2026} analyse pricing, entry and asymmetric platform spillovers. \citet{Lee2026} further establish that local knowledge spillovers and wider oligopoly rivalry can reverse the sign of an optimal R\&D policy. We therefore claim neither a new screening principle nor a new public-good externality or business-stealing theorem. The narrower question is what happens when fees determine \emph{both} match numbers and ability-weighted exposure, successful matched ventures have jurisdiction-specific market incidence, and four provision choices are independently determined. Section~\ref{sec:literature} compares the nearest contributions and records the limits of this distinction.

The institutions are not hypothetical without motivation. The debate over publicly supported British Catapult centres has raised the possibility of competition for projects and funding \citep{UKCommittee2026,UKGovernment2026}, while European Digital Innovation Hubs provide subsidised assistance alongside market-based valuation practices \citep{EuropeanCommissionEDIH,EDIHFAQ2026}. Neither case is evidence for the model's quantitative equilibrium. Moreover, the government here chooses a \emph{basic intermediation service}, not a publicly owned building, and the restricted planner cannot choose subsidies, procurement or digital-only alternatives. The paper thus offers a diagnostic framework for participant composition and the geographic incidence of innovation, not a prescription to construct a physical hub.

The remainder presents the model and backward induction, derives the conditional mechanisms, establishes the numerical entry comparisons and their fragility, and discusses closest research and institutional interpretation. Proof details, including the baseline-specific global private/private fee test, appear in the Appendix.
