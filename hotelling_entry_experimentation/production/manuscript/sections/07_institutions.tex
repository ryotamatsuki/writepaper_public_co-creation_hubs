\section{Institutional interpretation and policy boundaries}\label{sec:institutions}
The British Catapult debate exposes a live tension between public innovation support and private market activity. In March 2026, a House of Commons committee raised concerns that Catapult centres could compete with local organisations for projects and funding, especially where regional R\&D activity was weak \citep{UKCommittee2026}. The British government's June 2026 response defended mixed public grants, competitively secured research funding and commercial revenues as sustaining market responsiveness and limiting displacement \citep{UKGovernment2026}. Neither position is a causal demonstration of crowding out or crowding in. Catapult centres are hybrid organisations and cannot literally be equated with our independent free government entrant $G$ and profit-maximising private entrant $P$.

The European Digital Innovation Hubs network supplies another motivation. It provides regional access to technical experimentation, expertise and ecosystem connections while enabling cross-region specialist services \citep{EuropeanCommissionEDIH}. Its operational pricing guidance allows free or discounted assistance alongside market-price-based list values \citep{EDIHFAQ2026}. Hence zero-priced access and privately priced services may be relevant to the same institutional design; this does not mean every EDIH is state-operated or always free.

A concrete example of capability complementarity is the US Manufacturing Extension Partnership's reported support for a Montana company developing a fibre-optic cable clamp. The publicly supported extension network helped the inventors identify contract-manufacturing capabilities for commercialisation \citep{NISTFiberReaper2024}. This illustrates invention-implementation matching but does not estimate an equilibrium treatment effect.
Actual extension advice may directly improve technology, whereas our premium service changes direct service value and participation only; that function must not be inferred from the model.

The model's public alternative is a \emph{taxpayer-financed basic service}, not necessarily a physical building. The government finances the real fixed service cost and evaluates regional founder, incumbent and consumer outcomes. Since taxation, building technologies, procurement, subsidies and digital-only matching alternatives are not strategic choices, the study offers diagnostic questions---which skills join, where are new venture profits earned, who loses from incumbent displacement, and how do local incentives diverge from aggregate effects?---rather than a universal construction or ownership recommendation.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
The main result is a conditional screening--incidence inversion: greater technical complementarity can increase the economic value of free basic innovation intermediation even when additional successful ventures \emph{reduce} their financing region's product-market-plus-founder surplus. The primitive threshold \eqref{eq:screen-inversion-band} specifies a nonempty intermediate region of local venture-profit appropriation where this occurs. With weak matching it follows analytically and uniformly over an admissible compact technology interval; with the original finite matching intensity, exact rational interval arithmetic validates negative regional venture incidence, positive public willingness to pay, and increasing access value over a continuous set of profit shares and technology parameters. This is the central applied-theory result, rather than the mere possibility that a binary provider configuration changes.

The economic mechanism has two sides. Higher skill complementarity makes higher-ability founders more attractive to a fee-setting private specialist, increasing the optimally chosen admission cutoff. A public basic service restores the direct real use value lost when those founders are excluded, even though the additional matches can reduce local venture-related surplus through incumbent displacement and the location of profits. Prices are local transfers in the welfare accounting, so this is an allocation problem rather than a bookkeeping gain from moving fees between citizens and intermediaries. The private specialist's premium does not independently improve research technology; the government values basic access, not an assumed innovation-productivity gain from constructing a facility.

A wider valid parameter region for the \emph{conditional public-value slope} must not be confused with a robust \emph{four-player equilibrium ownership switch}. The three original entry equilibria remain strictly certified under their stated benchmarks and a narrow government fixed-cost band. Their switch is small relative to the cost and fails the recorded Beta$(2,2)$ skill alternative. No general first-stage comparative static, optimal subsidy programme or claim that a physical hub dominates other public instruments is inferred.

For an empirically meaningful evaluation, the relevant measurements are the population of eligible founders and their pre-service capabilities, posted private fees, basic and premium use, matched-pair success and failure, the location of assigned founder profits, consumer surplus and local incumbent displacement. These identify distinct mechanisms rather than counting successful projects as a sufficient statistic for regional welfare. The result joins familiar matching-price selection and interjurisdictional welfare accounting in a precise conditional policy comparison; it does not establish a new general principle of public-sector ownership or theoretical priority over all existing nonlinear network models.
