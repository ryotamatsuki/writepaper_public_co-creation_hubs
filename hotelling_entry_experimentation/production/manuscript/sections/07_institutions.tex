\section{Discussion: policy interpretation and limits}\label{sec:institutions}

The economic lesson concerns what a government compares, not a blanket presumption in favour of public provision. Where a paid intermediary selects founders, the relevant counterfactual is welfare under private-only service versus welfare when free basic access is added while a paid premium remains available. The latter arrangement restores basic participation but also changes premium-service use and the downstream incidence of additional ventures. Consequently, a negative local return from commercialisation does not settle whether greater technical complementarity raises the value of restoring access. Proposition~1 establishes the circumstances under which this sign conflict is possible; it does not say that locally harmful ventures are desirable or that subsidised access is always justified. The government must still compare the gross access value with the real public fixed cost. The corresponding national comparison additionally counts the partner region's welfare increment, which may have either sign.

For policy appraisal, this distinction puts the eligible population and the private-market counterfactual ahead of counts of projects supported. The model suggests separating the founders excluded by the private fee, the direct value of basic and premium services, the regional consumer and incumbent effects of new products, and the allocation of venture earnings between regions. Private fee receipts paid by local founders are transfers within the local welfare account, whereas the real costs of supplying services enter welfare. These are components of a diagnostic, not estimates furnished by the present paper. Observing a successful project, a rising participation rate or an increase in the posted specialist fee would not by itself identify the welfare threshold or the causal screening mechanism. The data requirements and identification difficulties are described in Section~\ref{sec:empirical}.

Existing institutions illustrate the relevance of this comparison without verifying the model's predictions. The European Digital Innovation Hubs network supports regional access to experimentation, expertise and cross-region specialists; its guidance allows free or discounted services alongside market-price-based valuations \citep{EuropeanCommissionEDIH,EDIHFAQ2026}. The United Kingdom's Catapult debate similarly makes the relationship between publicly supported innovation services and other local providers salient: a parliamentary committee raised concerns about competition for projects and funding, while the government's response emphasised the role of mixed funding and commercial revenues \citep{UKCommittee2026,UKGovernment2026}. These arguments are not causal evidence of either displacement or complementarity. Moreover, Catapult centres are hybrid institutions, not necessarily the model's separate free public and profit-maximising private providers. The US Manufacturing Extension Partnership's reported assistance to a Montana inventor in identifying contract-manufacturing capabilities illustrates the invention--implementation matching problem \citep{NISTFiberReaper2024}, but not the fee-induced reversal proved here.

The limits matter for interpretation. Proposition~1 assumes uniform, perfectly aligned skill and premium-service valuations, an interior private fee, full participation in the partner region and sufficiently weak matching for its analytical sign result. Premium advice has direct service value but does not improve research productivity, and pairing is not a model of purposeful partner search. The finite-matching certificate covers a specified continuous parameter region, not arbitrary economic environments. The separately certified four-party provision switch occupies a narrow fixed-cost band and disappears under the reported Beta$(2,2)$ alternative; the recorded adverse differentiated-Bertrand exercise is not independently re-established as a new theorem. Neither a general equilibrium response nor a quantitatively important welfare change in an observed economy follows.

Finally, the government's instrument is taxpayer-financed \emph{basic intermediation}, not a physical innovation hub. Building costs and location, capacity or service quality, taxation, subsidies, procurement and remote alternatives are not choices in the game. The analysis consequently cannot decide whether a region should construct a facility or which support instrument is optimal. Its narrower contribution is to identify when the evaluation of access must account simultaneously for endogenous private screening and potentially adverse regional commercialisation incidence, rather than rely on the sign of either component alone.
