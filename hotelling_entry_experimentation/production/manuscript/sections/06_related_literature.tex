\section{Related literature and contribution boundary}\label{sec:literature}

The proposed contribution is not that screening, knowledge spillovers, product-market displacement, or local-public underprovision can occur. Each has established predecessors. We instead ask whether a specific \emph{equilibrium chain}---private fees selecting the number and skills of prospective founders, bilateral commercial innovation, asymmetric regional product-market incidence, and independent public/private entry---can change the composition of intermediation services. The chain is illustrated in a restricted parameter environment; we do not assert that none of its steps can be obtained by specialising a broader prior framework.

\paragraph{Public/private supply, fees and endogenous participation.}
\citet{HuaChenZhang2016} examine a two-tier public service system with free public provision, paid private services and government fiscal coordination. \citet{LaineMa2017} study heterogeneous demand, mixed public/private quality and price competition, while \citet{Kim2024} study mixed two-sided platform provision. Free and paid tiers, coexistence, and ownership-dependent service prices are therefore prior art. \citet{LongWong2009} already place two public authorities alongside private local firms with strategic pricing and public investment. Our game additionally makes \emph{both} public and \emph{both} private entry choices binary and independent, but counting four institutional players is not an independent theorem. Its distinctive endogenous payoff state is the pair of founder aggregates $(m_i,q_i)$, which changes the profitability of cross-region ventures and their product-market consequences.

\citet{Hagiu2004,Hagiu2004b} analyse proprietary/open platforms, prices, vertical heterogeneity and a variety--quality welfare trade-off, with developer quality and investment already endogenous in the latter study. In particular, Hagiu's Propositions~2--3 make it inappropriate to claim a new general trade-off between the quantity and quality of participants. \citet{PeitzSato2026} analyse asymmetric platform pricing, entry and network spillovers with partial participation; \citet{LuMatsushimaShekhar2025} also show that developer fees and consumer welfare can react nontrivially to personalised platform pricing. We use the familiar monopoly selection mechanism, but distinguish a selected unnormalised skill moment $q_i$ from founder mass $m_i$ because commercial success depends on $q_Aq_B$, not simply on the number of customers or their conditional mean skill.

\paragraph{Innovation networks, new products and national incidence.}
\citet{BerliantFujita2008,BerliantFujita2011} analyse knowledge heterogeneity and production of new knowledge; \citet{DAspremontJacquemin1988,GoyalJoshi2003} connect R\&D collaboration and strategic competition. The effects of new technologies on product-market rivalry are not new either. A useful recent near-prior is \citet{Lee2026}, who analyse cost-reducing R\&D with exogenous knowledge-spillover networks in Cournot oligopoly and derive a spillover/network threshold at which the optimal \emph{R\&D tax or subsidy} changes sign. Their decomposition explicitly weighs market expansion, local knowledge spillovers and wider competition; it thus absorbs any broad claim that such countervailing welfare forces are newly identified here. Unlike their subsidy/tax choice, our instrument is a local binary decision to finance basic \emph{intermediation}; founders' selection into cross-region ventures responds endogenously to intermediary fees, while firm R\&D effort has the separable continuation stated in \eqref{eq:effort}. We do not derive a superior tax or subsidy rule, and the present P1 regional-incidence derivative is not a substitute for Lee's policy threshold.

For a close applied-theory comparison, \citet{HacknerHerzing2026} characterise strategic adoption of cost-reducing technologies under competitive or patented technology supply and compare private and social adoption in Cournot markets. Their result concerns the number of adopting producers and provision of cost-reducing technology, not ability-selected bilateral founder matching and the entry incentives of two governments and two specialist intermediaries. The example is evidence that equilibrium technology provision is a relevant applied IO question; it does not confer originality or robustness on our numerical example.

\paragraph{Decentralised public decisions and welfare benchmark.}
\citet{CremerMarchandPestieau1997} analyse Nash versus socially optimal local public investment with cross-jurisdictional spillovers, and \citet{MankiwWhinston1986} provide classic private-entry welfare inefficiency results. Our conditional Proposition~3 is a decomposition of those familiar externality considerations: the two-region threshold exceeds the local threshold exactly by the neighbouring region's surplus increment. It is not an independent general underprovision theorem. The constrained planner compares the same sixteen provision choices under decentralised downstream continuations; it does not choose a first-best research allocation, optimal subsidies or an institutional contracting instrument. The unpublished discussion paper of \citet{KawasakiMatsushima2026} further establishes non-monotone public/private welfare comparisons in a model with endogenous data collection and pricing; neither non-monotonicity nor ownership-sensitive welfare rankings should be presented as discoveries of this model.

\paragraph{Empirical context is not causal identification.}
\citet{NishimuraOkamuro2026} discuss associations between Japanese place-based R\&D assistance, intermediary support and firms' innovation outcomes. The UK Catapult debate and European Digital Innovation Hubs similarly illustrate coexistence and potential displacement, but do not measure the model's ability correlation, cross-region skill moments, fee responses or four-player entry counterfactual. Their role is institutional motivation rather than empirical validation.

\subsection{The bounded integrated finding}\label{sec:contribution}
The central finding is that equal full-participation mean innovation success does not guarantee an equal \emph{decentralised intermediation configuration} once fees select technically complementary founders and venture gains are regionally distributed. At $\lambda=0.5$ the selected baseline skill model yields $(B,B)$, whereas the homogeneous full-population-mean alternative yields $(B,P)$; both are benchmark-specific equilibria, not a generic ownership ordering. The conditional fee/cutoff, market-incidence and local-versus-national public-addition identities explain the economic chain, while a baseline-specific globally certified P/P continuation checks that the result is not dependent on a fee-grid-local optimum. The opposite ownership result occurs in a narrow public-cost interval and fails under the recorded $\operatorname{Beta}(2,2)$ alternative.
