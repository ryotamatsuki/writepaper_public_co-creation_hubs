\begin{abstract}
We examine when public basic innovation access is worthwhile alongside
fee-charging private specialists in two jurisdictions. A five-stage model
combines private founder screening, complementary cross-region research,
and differentiated Cournot competition. Conditional local and national
public-cost ceilings account separately for recovered basic-service value
and downstream regional commercialisation incidence. We identify an
intermediate founder-profit-share band in which greater technical
complementarity raises the local public-access cost ceiling despite
negative local returns from additional ventures. An analytic
general-distribution extension characterises the corresponding threshold
under regular ability distributions. A frozen-fee counterfactual reverses
the positive slope, identifying the private repricing channel. A
constructive Cournot counterexample holds private fees, participation,
success and venture profits fixed yet reverses the public-value slope by
changing regional consumer and incumbent incidence. Exact rational
certificates verify the conditional sign for continuous finite-matching
ranges under uniform and Beta$(2,2)$ abilities. Separately certified
four-party entry examples include public underprovision relative to a
restricted planner, but their configuration switch requires a narrow
government-cost band and fails under the reported alternative
distribution. The results offer conditional appraisal diagnostics, not a
general entry-equilibrium theorem, robust quantitative policy forecast,
or case for constructing physical facilities.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Innovation intermediation; Public access; Participant selection; Mixed provision; Regional welfare.
