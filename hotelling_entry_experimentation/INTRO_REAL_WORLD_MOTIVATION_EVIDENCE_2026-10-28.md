# Stage 33 — International real-world motivation evidence for the Introduction

**Research date:** 2026-10-28 (JST)  
**Artifact role:** empirical/institutional illustration and introduction-writing evidence; **NOT a modification to frozen Stage 8 theory**.  
**Freeze authority:** `STAGE33-2026-10-28-THEORY-FREEZE-v1`, `STAGE8_CANONICAL_THEORY_FREEZE_V1.md`.  
**Research question:** When, alongside privately supplied paid innovation intermediation, is taxpayer-funded free intermediation worth providing locally, given heterogeneous participants, interregional collaboration and regional/national welfare divergence?

## Executive recommendation

**Lead with the 2026 UK Catapult policy controversy (strongest public-versus-private and regional/national tension), illustrate the real matching channel with NIST MEP small business cases (most intuitive for general readers), and cite the EU EDIH network as evidence that free/subsidized local hubs and cross-region expertise are sizable live policy institutions.** Germany's free vendor-neutral Mittelstand-Digital and Fraunhofer's hybrid funding provide institutional counterpoints rather than direct instances of the full model.

All described cases are *motivation, analogies and observed institutional design*, not empirical tests of Stage33's Nash equilibrium, local welfare signs, or exogenous skill/taste correlation.

## A. Best opening puzzle: UK Parliament and Government disagree about regional Catapult intermediaries (2026)

**Primary source A1:** UK House of Commons, Science, Innovation and Technology Committee, *Flying Blind: Innovation, Growth and the Regions*, Third Report of Session 2024–26, **13 March 2026**, paras **80–84**, and in particular paras 81–84: https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5901/cmselect/cmsctech/538/report.html

**Verified observations:** Catapults are not-for-profit technology and innovation centres supported by Innovate UK. They are meant to bridge research and industry. The committee reported testimony that core-funding conditions requiring Catapults to obtain private and other competitive income might force competition with local firms and universities for projects and grants, and potentially attract staff away from SMEs. It concluded that in low-R&D regions, Catapults risk competing with local bodies for funds rather than diffusing knowledge. It recommended investigating whether loosening requirements to secure private income would improve regional impact and collecting region-level commercialisation and economic impact measures. The report stated 74% of Innovate UK's core Catapult funding went outside the Greater South East. This is **a committee report of evidence and policy criticism**, not an identified causal effect.

**Primary source A2 (essential opposing position):** UK House of Commons Committee, *Flying Blind: Government Response*, **16 June 2026**, response to paras 82–84: https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5902/cmselect/cmsctech/271/report.html

**Verified government counterargument:** Catapults are national translational infrastructures rather than instruments whose primary mandate is local economic development; public base grants mixed with commercial income and competitive R&D funding, the government argues, keep organisations market-responsive and avoid displacement of private firms. It rejects removing the private-revenue requirement and states that facilities should be located where industrial capability, research strength and market opportunities already exist, rather than indiscriminately in lagging areas. This is **a policy position**, not independent proof against displacement.

**Why this is especially strong for an international IO/regional economics Introduction:** A factual, dated, explicit disagreement over whether publicly supported innovation brokers complement or crowd out private provision, and whether national commercialisation objectives align with local regional outcomes. It leads naturally to a model where **public G maximises local W, private P maximises profit, and a restricted planner evaluates W_A+W_B**. The actual Catapult entity combines public and commercial revenues; it is **not** literally independent free G versus private P, nor necessarily a profit-maximising P.

**Allowed wording:** “A 2026 UK parliamentary inquiry warned that public innovation centres might compete with local firms and universities for resources, while the government's response defended mixed public–private financing as a way to sustain market discipline and limit displacement.” Cite both publications.

**Disallowed:** “The UK proved that Catapults crowd out private innovation”; “the government's interpretation was proved correct”; “Catapults operate as free government hubs competing with a distinct for-profit intermediary”; “the regional/national welfare sign is empirically measured by this report.”

## B. Closest free/public provision and interregional capability analogy: European Digital Innovation Hubs (EDIHs)

**B1 EC official program overview:** European Commission, *European Digital Innovation Hubs*: https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/edihs — hubs are local one-stop services for SMEs and public bodies providing technical testing (“test before invest”), training, finance navigation and ecosystem access. Crucially, the EC explicitly notes a **regional presence** plus the ability to offer **specialised services across regions** when skills are unavailable locally. Earlier calls were 50% financed by Digital Europe and 50% by member states/regions and/or private sources. Do not infer that every service is always free or that the hub is state-operated.

**B2 2026 operational context:** European Digital Innovation Hubs Network, *Europe's digital Innovation hubs: now active in 40 countries*, **7 September 2026**, https://european-digital-innovation-hubs.ec.europa.eu/sv/node/402674 — says network spans **40 countries**, with **more than €1.2bn co-invested since 2023** by Commission, countries, regions and partners. Services are **free or discounted**, not invariably free. €1.2bn is **co-investment in the initiative**, not a causal estimate of program benefit.

**B3 Important institutional pricing detail (preferred live FAQ, updated 4 August 2026):** EDIH Network, *FAQ: European Digital Innovation Hubs*, section **E.1/E.2**, https://european-digital-innovation-hubs.ec.europa.eu/me/node/19884. Hubs should have **a list price grounded in market prices** where available and explicitly record reduced/zero fees as assistance to eligible firms. FAQ gives an **illustrative hypothetical**, not an actual evaluated case, of robot testing with list price **€500 per day** offered for €0, and an alternative price of €250; note these numbers are a **worked example**, NOT observed prices. This is unusually clear direct institutional acknowledgment that subsidized/free access and market pricing must be reconciled.

**B4 Draft 2025 FAQ corroboration, WEAKER authority:** `https://european-digital-innovation-hubs.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2025-03/250312_FAQ.pdf`, dated 12 March 2025, **DRAFT** V0.6, p.7, Q.F.4, explicitly warns against distorting market prices regionally. It is an explanatory draft, **not legally binding**, and the live FAQ B3 is the better primary cite. We checked screenshot page index 6 (printed page 7).

**B5 EDIH program activities evaluation:** EC JRC, *European Digital Innovation Hubs Network's activities and customers: State of play report 2024*, published **11 April 2025**, DOI `10.2760/7784020`, official landing page https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/32fa9035-167d-11f0-b1a3-01aa75ed71a1/language-en and JRC https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/jrc-news-and-updates/speeding-digitalisation-and-ai-uptake-european-businesses-2025-04-11_en. As at September 2024 the network held **more than 5,000 events**, reached **200,000 participants**, delivered **more than 18,000 services**; roughly 90% of European regions were covered in that measurement period. JRC reports 90% of surveyed firms improved their digital-maturity score after intervention. None of these figures identifies a counterfactual *causal* welfare effect; do not extrapolate 2024 counts to 2026.

**Fit to Stage33:** Real public finance enables low/zero access price and broader participation, while high-quality technical partners and interregional skills matter. A beneficiary's free access is not evidence of a rival P's endogenous optimal fee or observed sorting.

## C. One tangible jointly developed product example: German EDIH Saxony × SITEC × N+P (2025)

**Primary source C1:** European Digital Innovation Hubs Network, *Co-Innovating the Future of Manufacturing – How SITEC and EDIH Saxony Made Manufacturing-X a Reality*, **30 September 2025**: https://european-digital-innovation-hubs.ec.europa.eu/knowledge-hub/success-stories/co-innovating-future-manufacturing-how-sitec-and-edih-saxony-made

**Verified:** German medium-sized manufacturing/laser-technology firm SITEC of Chemnitz, its established IT-system partner N+P Informationssysteme, and EDIH Saxony/Smart Systems Hub collaborated in mixed teams of engineers, software developers and business experts during a three-day hackathon, webinars and testing facilities. They worked on machine-data interoperability, digital twins and a **prototype** of a Pay-per-Use business model for manufacturing equipment. This concretely exemplifies **complementary hardware/manufacturing and digital expertise** joined by an intermediary. The report records product/system development and a prototype—not verified net social welfare, a launched full-market product, a randomized counterfactual, or competitors' losses.

**Fit:** A founder's technical ability is not the same as the number of firms participating; skill composition of partners matters. Innovation intermediary can join providers with distinct skills. This is project-level matching but not proof of an interregional A–B pair (case is situated in Saxony).

**Allowed one-sentence vignette:** “In Germany, EDIH Saxony brought together manufacturing specialists and software experts to prototype data-enabled industrial services.” Cite case.

## D. US Manufacturing Extension Partnership: public-private fee-financing plus private partners

**D1 NIST official system description:** NIST, *About NIST MEP*, https://www.nist.gov/mep/about-nist-mep (reviewed 2026-10-28). The Hollings Manufacturing Extension Partnership (MEP) is a federal/state/local-private cost-shared network serving manufacturing SMEs through designated centres. NIST states federal appropriations fund **half**, with the rest from state/local and/or private entities **and client fees**. Centers partner with universities, labs, and private experts, assist product development, technology transfer and supplier matching. This is **hybrid public/private financing**, not uniformly free; distinct from the four independent strategic entrants in Stage33.

**D2 Direct venture manufacturer-supplier matching:** NIST, *Get a Grip: Fiber Reaper and MMEC Partner on Innovative Cable Clamp*, published **25 April 2024**, https://www.nist.gov/mep/successstories/2024/get-grip-fiber-reaper-and-mmec-partner-innovative-cable-clamp. A two-person Montana startup invented a fibre-optic cable clamp but lacked manufacturing and patent-commercialisation capacity. The publicly supported Montana MEP centre located and screened prospective **contract manufacturers**, advised on production design, and supported patent steps. NIST reports **$200,000 cost savings** and **$50,000 new investment** as its program success metrics; these are **client/program-reported outputs**, not verified causal effects. Perfect simple general-audience vignette of why invention skills and actual manufacturing skills must be paired.

**D3 Crucial counterweight — public broker can COMPLEMENT a private specialist:** NIST, *Engineering Success: How STYP, LLC Expanded Its Reach with Manufacturing Works*, https://www.nist.gov/mep/successstories/2024/engineering-success-how-styp-llc-expanded-its-reach-manufacturing-works. STYP LLC is a small, privately operated engineering/product-development service provider in Wyoming. MEP centre Manufacturing Works **referred clients needing engineering/design services to STYP**, expanding its client base; NIST reports **$44,000 new sales** and **$17,000 retained sales**, figures are case-program outputs. In this case a publicly supported intermediary generated demand for a **private paid expertise provider**, not necessarily displacement. No information about fee optimization or legal public subsidy superiority.

**Optional D4 alternative story:** TechHelp / Renegade Marine Jet Drive, *Propelling Forward with 3D Scanning*, https://www.nist.gov/mep/successstories/2024/propelling-forward-3d-scanning : NPD Lab converted hand-crafted boat impeller to CAD, prototypes and searched casting suppliers after original New Zealand artisan retired. Strong cross-border skill scarcity and commercialization case; weaker than D2 for clean firm-to-firm matching.

**What these cases say:** innovation brokers solve concrete “invention–production–commercialisation” coordination problems and sometimes integrate existing paid consultants instead of displacing them. They do **not** establish Stage33's specific private-profit function, relative social welfare magnitude, or unique mixed-provision equilibrium.

## E. German free, vendor-neutral alternative: Mittelstand-Digital

**E1 Primary source:** German Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Energy (BMWE), *Mittelstand bei der Digitalisierung unterstützen*, https://www.bundeswirtschaftsministerium.de/Redaktion/DE/Dossier/MittelstandDigital/foerderschwerpunkt-mittelstanddigital.html : federal funding ensures SMEs, crafts and startups **free use** of practical knowledge-transfer, digitalisation, AI and cybersecurity offers.

**E2 Announced next phase, NOT yet running as of research date:** BMWE press release **20 October 2026**, https://www.bundeswirtschaftsministerium.de/Redaktion/DE/Pressemitteilungen/2026/10/20261020-netzwerk-mittelstand-digital-zentren.html : from **1 January 2027** the new network is planned to open, with **six regional + eleven specialized** topic/industry centres by mid-2027; offers will be free and **vendor-neutral**. Do not state these 17 are already operating on 28 October 2026.

**Fit:** strongest real-world analogy to regionally accessible taxpayer-financed **free basic service**, but free advice is not the same as commercialised JV output. Not evidence that private advisory firms were crowded out.

## F. Third institutional design: Fraunhofer is a non-profit hybrid research intermediary, not a private entrant

**F1 Primary source:** Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft, *Finances* (2025 data, site updated 2026), https://www.fraunhofer.de/en/about-fraunhofer/profile-structure/facts-and-figures/finances.html. Germany's non-profit applied-research organisation combines base funding from federal and state governments with competitive public and industrial R&D contracts and licensing. Institutional base funding is at most about one-third. In **2025**, external project funding share was **71.1%** (definition is operating budget share), industrial revenue **€966m**; total business volume **€3.6bn**, contract research **€3.2bn**. Distinguish these different budget denominators; never add percentages from different definitions.

**Fit:** shows why the stark public G/private P dichotomy is an analytical decomposition of **financing, access pricing and provider incentives**, not a literal description of every institution. Fraunhofer is non-profit, so should NOT be coded as profit-maximizing P. Better second paragraph/footnote rather than main hook.

## G. What the empirical record DOES NOT show

1. No cited programme documents a four-player Nash equilibrium or the Stage33 root `lambda*=0.351184...` in observed data.
2. No cited programme establishes the assumed perfect positive sorting of technical skill and demand for premium private services (`z_i=theta_i`). This is a **structural modelling assumption**, not an observed institutional regularity.
3. EU, UK and US programmes differ in ownership and financial contracts: Catapults/Fraunhofer/MEP can be subsidized and charge fees simultaneously. Do not equate an institution with exactly one pure G or P strategic entrant.
4. No cited case directly measures **old-product consumer surplus + incumbent-profit displacement** from introduced new venture products. Stage33's `M_i` and `K_i` are theoretical objects, not measured programme outcomes.
5. UK 2026 committee/Government statements are **opposing institutional assessments**. Their disagreement motivates the question; neither demonstrates a clean causal inference.
6. NIST and EDIH individual success stories are **selected promotional/institutional reports**, not randomized estimates or proof of additionality. JRC maturity scores are before/after, not necessarily program-caused.
7. EDIH pricing examples in the general FAQ are **hypothetical compliance examples**, not price observations. The 2025 supplementary FAQ is explicitly **DRAFT**; use the 2026 main FAQ and regulation for hard administrative facts.
8. Public provision in the paper denotes publicly funded free access; the paper does not establish the indispensability of physical floorspace, comparative advantage over subsidies/contracts, or demonstrated effects in a particular prefecture.
9. The timing and data periods matter: EDIH 2024 evaluation counts ≠ 2026 current network; planned Germany 2027 structure is not operating in October 2026.

## H. Recommended Introduction architecture (economics journal)

**Paragraph 1 — the observed policy puzzle:** lead with UK 2026 committee vs Government debate and one compact EU EDIH fact, not a list of national initiatives. Establish two competing effects: support for otherwise unrealised innovation matches vs displacement and misallocation of local public resources.

**Paragraph 2 — tangible mechanism:** briefly introduce a two-person startup that invented a cable clamp but needed manufacturing partners (NIST Montana), and optionally the public broker referred customers to a paid private engineer (Wyoming). Emphasize both *matching across heterogeneous capabilities* and *complementarity/substitution with private specialists*. Do not claim these empirical cases establish the model's perfect skill/taste association.

**Paragraph 3 — economic question/gap:** why do *number AND type of firms participating*, privately set prices, regional distribution of JV value and downstream business-stealing change the local and national ranking of zero/private/free/both institutional provision? Existing Hagiu and other platform theory studies are cited as prior mechanism literature, not mislabeled as having overlooked a problem proven absent from their models.

**Paragraph 4 — model setup and results:** the frozen Stage33 four-entrant game; P2's sorting, P3's bounded welfare wedge, and P4's baseline switch as a model-specific existence result, plus robustness failure in Beta/Bertrand.

**Paragraph 5 — strict policy interpretation:** taxpayer-financed low/zero basic access can coexist with paid speciality service; the theory identifies distortions, not an implementable procurement/subsidy scheme or physical-location necessity.

### Candidate English opening paragraph (DRAFT; references to the primary reports, not outcome claims)

> Whether publicly supported innovation intermediaries complement or displace private activity remains a live policy question. In 2026, a UK parliamentary inquiry warned that Catapult centres could compete with local firms and universities for funds instead of diffusing innovation, whereas the government's response defended their combination of public funding and private revenues as a means of preserving market discipline. At the same time, the European Union's Digital Innovation Hubs provide free or discounted technical support while connecting local firms to specialised expertise across regions. These institutions highlight a basic economic tension: lowering barriers to innovation collaboration may enlarge the set of viable projects, but it may also reallocate business away from existing service providers, and the location of the benefits need not coincide with the location of the public cost. [UK Committee 2026; UK Government Response 2026; EC EDIHs].

### Candidate second paragraph (DRAFT; real example remains program-reported)

> The matching problem extends well beyond access to research laboratories. A two-person startup in Montana developed a novel cable clamp but needed help finding capable contract manufacturers and navigating commercialization; the federally supported Manufacturing Extension Partnership connected it with relevant production expertise. In another US case, a publicly supported MEP centre referred engineering work to a privately operated design firm. Such examples illustrate why public and private intermediation can be complements as well as competitors, and why the capabilities of the participating firms may matter as much as their number. [NIST MEP 2024, Fiber Reaper; NIST MEP 2024, STYP].

Neither draft asserts observed causal effects, fee-selection elasticity, net social efficiency, or proof that one named institution exactly instantiates all four model agents.
