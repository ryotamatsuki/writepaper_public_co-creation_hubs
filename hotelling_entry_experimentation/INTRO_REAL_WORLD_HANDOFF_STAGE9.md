## One preferred, internationally intelligible entry point

**Begin with the disagreement between the UK Parliament and UK Government in 2026 over publicly funded Catapult research-commercialisation centres**, then introduce EU EDIHs as a broad, concrete international programme. The economic question is not “Should public hubs exist?” in the abstract, but “Under what conditions do publicly funded free/basic innovation services complement rather than displace private provision, when participating firms' capabilities and the downstream effects of commercialisation are heterogeneous across regions?”

- House of Commons Committee (13 March 2026), paras 80–84: https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5901/cmselect/cmsctech/538/report.html
- Government response (16 June 2026): https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5902/cmselect/cmsctech/271/report.html
- European Commission EDIH network: https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/edihs

### Candidate opening A: concise, editorial-policy puzzle (recommended)

> Can publicly supported innovation intermediaries expand collaboration without displacing private providers? The tension has become explicit in European innovation policy. In March 2026, a UK parliamentary committee raised concerns that publicly supported Catapult centres may compete with local firms and universities for resources, rather than simply spreading technical knowledge. The government's response defended the same centres' mixed public-and-commercial funding model as essential to remaining responsive to market demand. Meanwhile, the EU has built a cross-regional network of Digital Innovation Hubs providing free or discounted support to firms seeking specialist expertise. These developments pose a basic question: when should governments support innovation intermediation alongside privately supplied services, and how should the answer depend on the composition of participating firms and the location of the resulting gains and losses?

**Citation placements:** first two UK claims cite separate UK official sources; EU claim cite EC overview plus current EDIH Network 7 Sept 2026 report. The EU reference is descriptive, not causal. *“alongside privately supplied services”* is the theoretical question, not an assertion each of these schemes has exactly two distinct competing centres.

### Candidate opening B: micro-level startup first, then public-private policy

> A small inventor may possess an attractive technical idea but lack the manufacturing expertise needed to bring it to market. In Montana, a two-person startup developing a fibre-optic cable clamp turned to a publicly supported Manufacturing Extension Partnership centre for help locating contract manufacturers and preparing production. In Wyoming, the same federal–state extension network helped a privately operated engineering consultancy find customers that needed its design capabilities. These examples illustrate two potential functions of innovation intermediation: assembling complementary technical capabilities and connecting businesses to private specialist services. Whether public provision reinforces such market connections or supplants them depends on incentives that are not visible from successful projects alone.

**Citation placements:** NIST Fiber Reaper and NIST STYP case reports. Note neither establishes causal impact, a pure free G, the four-player game, or the technological skill distribution.

### Candidate paragraph C: economist's bridge from institutions to mechanisms

> An intermediary affects more than the number of collaborations. By selecting participants according to their willingness to pay, a private provider may change the distribution of technical capabilities among the firms that engage in joint R&D. Subsidized basic access can alter that selection, while the resulting new products can generate gains for project participants and consumers but losses for incumbent producers. Because those effects need not accrue in the same jurisdiction as the public financing, local provision incentives can diverge from the aggregate welfare ranking. We develop a two-region entry game with local governments and private intermediaries to characterize these linked mechanisms.

**Crucial distinction:** paragraph C is the **model's motivation and prediction**, NOT a fact established by UK / EDIH / NIST records. Must be referenced to the paper's formal propositions, not empirically attributed to case reports.

## Selection decision for Stage10

1. **Preferred primary story:** UK Parliament/Government 2026 tension. Authentic controversy; most editorially concise and clear for international audiences.
2. **Scale and service design:** EU EDIH regional/cross-regional service networks and the 2026 market-price-based list/discount scheme. Good direct G motivation but real EDIH services are not always free.
3. **Concrete individual firm:** Montana Fiber Reaper; optional short Wyoming STYP contrast to show a publicly supported network can refer business to private specialists.
4. **Second-tier example or footnote:** German EDIH Saxony–SITEC–N+P co-innovation prototype, Germany's free vendor-neutral Mittelstand-Digital.
5. **Footnote / scope defence:** Germany's Fraunhofer finances show hybrid organizations cannot mechanically be relabeled as pure government G or pure profit-maximizing private P.
